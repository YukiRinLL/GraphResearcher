# Ring of Nestor

**Ring of Nestor** is a name used for an Aegean gold ring whose imagery and authenticity have been debated in archaeological literature. A 2011 peer-reviewed article states that the ring was reportedly found in 1924, that Sir Arthur Evans accepted it as authentic, and that other scholars judged it a forgery; the same abstract reports later renewed claims of authenticity.[5] Theodore Eliopoulos published a 2013 study specifically on the ring’s iconography.[1]

The terminology is not uniform. An archival study uses “Minos Ring,” while one of its references uses “Pseudo-Minoan Nestor Ring”; the cited text does not explain whether those designations refer to the same object.[8] This naming difference is separate from the authenticity disagreement: the 2011 article reports Evans’s acceptance, opposing judgments of forgery, and later unnamed claims of authenticity, while the archival abstract reports Marinatos’s and Karo’s forgery assessment alongside a statement that later evidence suggested genuineness.[5][8] The ring is also distinct in the cited publications from the gold signet rings reported from the Griffin Warrior burial at Pylos, which are not identified as the Ring of Nestor.[15]

## Description and iconography

## Description and iconography

A 2013 article by Theodore Eliopoulos is specifically devoted to the iconography of the “Ring of Nestor.”[1] A separate study of Thessaloniki museum inventory MΘ 23748 describes an unknown-provenance gold signet ring with an elongated, almost wedge-shaped bezel, a flat-convex upper surface, a concave reverse, and a nearly circular plain hoop.[16] Its intaglio depicts two horned, hoofed quadrupeds in a mating scene, although their species cannot be identified securely.[16] That study does not equate MΘ 23748 with the Ring of Nestor; the physical description therefore remains attached to that inventory number.[16]

Separate Pylos publications describe gold signet rings from the Griffin Warrior burial. One displayed summary describes a ring with a seascape, a shrine flanked by palm trees, and five female figures; the summary does not call it the Ring of Nestor.[15] The cited Pylos material therefore distinguishes those Pylos rings from the ring discussed in the 2011 Nestor Ring study.[5][15]

The terminology presents a specific uncertainty: the cited archival study uses “Minos Ring,” while a reference title uses “Pseudo-Minoan Nestor Ring,” and the cited text does not explain whether those designations denote the same object.[8] Nanno Marinatos, an archaeologist and co-author with Briana Jackson of the 2011 peer-reviewed study, presents semantic coherence and Egyptian prototypes as part of that study’s interpretive framework; its abstract states that the butterfly motif argues against authenticity.[5] The names are therefore reported as source-specific designations rather than treated as proven synonyms.[1][8]

## Provenance and publication history

## Provenance and publication history

The documented archaeological setting associated with the name Nestor is the Palace of Nestor at Ano Englianos in Messenia. Excavations begun in 1939 uncovered the palace, and subsequent project publications address the history of investigations in Messenia, the palace, and its Linear B documents.[10][9][20] The palace excavation continued in annual campaigns from 1952 to 1964, while the palace itself was destroyed by fire at the end of the 13th century BCE.[19] These facts establish the context of Pylos archaeology, not the findspot or date of the Ring of Nestor.[9][10][19]

A 2025 publication describes exhibitions in Kalamata, Malibu, and Athens devoted to the wider Kingdom of Pylos, but the catalogue description does not identify the Ring of Nestor as an exhibited object or provide its ownership and custody history.[10] The cited Pylos publications likewise do not provide an object-level discovery or accession record for the named ring.[9][10]

The available publication listings therefore do not establish a continuous chronology of discovery, ownership, custody, acquisition, and publication for the Ring of Nestor.[9][10] The distinction between the Palace of Nestor and the separately named ring is maintained because the cited archaeological and exhibition records do not identify them as the same object.[10]

## Authenticity debate

## Authenticity debate

The authenticity of the object called the Ring of Nestor has not been resolved by the supplied sources. Nanno Marinatos, an archaeologist, and Briana Jackson report in a peer-reviewed 2011 article that the ring was found in 1924, that Sir Arthur Evans accepted it as authentic, and that many other scholars condemned it as a forgery.[5] The same abstract reports renewed claims of authenticity in later scholarship, but it does not name the scholars or give the evidence for those claims.[5] These statements document a history of competing assessments rather than independently proving either authenticity or forgery.

The 2011 study presents two arguments against authenticity. First, it judges the reported circumstances of discovery suspicious. Second, it states that the use of the butterfly motif argues against authenticity.[5] The supplied abstract does not specify the discovery circumstances, provide the comparative corpus, or reproduce the detailed iconographic analysis, so the strength of those arguments cannot be assessed here beyond the authors’ stated conclusion.

The same study’s framework gives a different kind of argument: Marinatos and Jackson state that semantic coherence depends on Egyptian prototypes.[5] This is an interpretive criterion, not a materials test or excavation record. The supplied material contains no authenticated scientific examination of the Ring of Nestor’s alloy, manufacture, tool marks, or engraving sequence. An automated summary of Theodore Eliopoulos’s 2013 article reports that a connection between incurved altars and the ring’s depiction supports authenticity, but the underlying article text and argument are not available in the supplied record.[1] That statement is consequently weaker evidence than the peer-reviewed article’s explicit abstract-level conclusions.

An archival study using the name “Minos Ring” adds a further, but not fully transferable, disagreement. Its abstract states that Spyridon Marinatos and Georg Karo regarded that ring as a forgery, while new evidence in an unavailable appendix was said to suggest that it was genuine.[8] The cited bibliography uses “Pseudo-Minoan Nestor Ring,” whereas the study’s title and abstract use “Minos Ring”; the supplied material does not establish that these names refer to the same object.[8] The authenticity positions can therefore be reported as part of the documented debate, but the available evidence does not justify combining them with other Pylos-related rings.[8]
