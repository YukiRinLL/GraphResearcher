The Ring of Nestor is a large gold signet ring, now in the Ashmolean Museum in Oxford, whose engraved bezel carries an elaborate multi‑scene composition of human figures, landscape motifs and hybrid creatures that Arthur Evans interpreted as a vision of a Minoan afterlife.[1][3][5] Evans first published the ring in 1925, presenting it as part of a "sepulchral treasure" of gold signet rings and bead‑seals reportedly found in 1915 in a Mycenaean rock‑cut tomb near the village of Dombrena by Thisbê in Boeotia, and dated the assemblage to a relatively advanced phase of the Mycenaean period on the basis of associated weapons and objects.[1][6] Later museum and popular descriptions generally classify the Ring of Nestor as a Late Minoan or Aegean Bronze Age work, often assigning it a date between about 1700 and 1450 BCE, and emphasize the complexity of its ritual iconography.[3][5][18]

Evans named the object "The Ring of Nestor" as a literary allusion to the Homeric king of Pylos, suggesting that such a richly decorated gold signet ring might be imagined as belonging to a figure like Nestor, but the name does not indicate an archaeological provenance from Pylos.[1] Since Evans’s publication, the ring has become a prominent and debated example in studies of Aegean Bronze Age glyptic, Minoan–Mycenaean religious imagery and the relationship between archaeological material and Homeric tradition.[1][2][11] Its authenticity and precise origin have been the subject of prolonged controversy: some scholars and institutional authors have listed or treated the Ring of Nestor as a suspect or possibly forged object, sometimes linking it to the restorer and suspected forger Émile Gilliéron fils,[3][4][5] while Ingo Pini and other specialists have argued, on the basis of comparative iconography, manufacturing techniques and wear, that the ring is consistent with securely provenanced Minoan–Mycenaean seals and should be regarded as genuine.[4][15]
