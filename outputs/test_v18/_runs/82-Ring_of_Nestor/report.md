# Ring of Nestor

The Ring of Nestor is the conventional name for a Cretan–Mycenaean gold signet ring identified in the supplied scholarly literature as CMS VI, no. 277. Its modern importance derives from the combination of its elaborate bezel imagery, its association with Minoan and Mycenaean Bronze Age art, and a continuing dispute over whether it is an ancient object or a modern forgery.[6][10] The ring is commonly reported as having been allegedly discovered in a tholos tomb at Kakovatos near Pylos, but the available evidence does not provide a professional excavation record or a secure chain of custody for that claim.[10]

Arthur Evans privately acquired the object and published it in 1925, interpreting the bezel scene as a Minoan afterlife or Elysian domain in which a human couple enters the Elysian Fields.[10] A later interpretation identifies Evans’s “watch-dog” or “dragon” motif as a scorpion, understood in that reading as an apotropaic figure that kills the ring’s “Minoan Dragon.”[6] These readings are interpretations of the imagery rather than settled identifications of every figure or motif.

The authenticity question remains unresolved in the supplied literature. An Athenian archaeological board reportedly regarded the ring as a forgery made by a modern artist before Evans purchased it.[10] A 2020 contribution by A. G. Vlachopoulos, an archaeologist writing on Aegean iconography, reports a reassessment of the Ring of Nestor and related Cretan–Mycenaean gold rings in favor of authenticity, drawing broadly on comparative imagery and study of craftsmanship.[6] At the same time, a 2011 review of CMS VI states that doubts about no. 277 persisted, so the object is best presented as contested rather than conclusively authenticated.[12]

## Discovery and provenance

The reported archaeological origin of the Ring of Nestor is an alleged discovery in a tholos tomb at Kakovatos near Pylos. The account places the ring’s first appearance in scholarly literature in 1925, but the wording itself marks the findspot as unverified and the supplied passage contains no excavation record, tomb number, associated inventory, or documented transfer from excavators to the modern market.[10] The term *gemma dubitanda* is relevant to this evidentiary status: in the usage documented by the supplied study, it denotes sealstones and signet rings presented as possible Aegean Bronze Age objects whose provenance or date is uncertain and whose alleged origins are not securely established.[10]

Kakovatos is an independently documented archaeological site with three tholos tombs excavated by Wilhelm Dörpfeld in two campaigns, in 1907 and 1908.[15] The available Kakovatos study records gold and other objects among the finds, but it does not identify an excavated object from those tombs as the Ring of Nestor or as CMS VI, no. 277.[15] The site’s documented excavation history therefore confirms that Kakovatos is an archaeological locality, not that the particular ring came from a controlled excavation there.

The secure modern publication trail begins with Arthur Evans. The supplied account states that Evans privately purchased the object and published it in the *Journal of Hellenic Studies* in 1925.[10] His related 1925 publication was also issued separately in London by Macmillan; the catalog record describes a 75-page work with five plates and notes that it was published by permission from an April 1925 *Journal of Hellenic Studies* paper.[9] These bibliographic and acquisition details document the object’s Evans-era appearance in scholarship, but they do not supply the seller, earlier owner, purchase documentation, or a complete chain of custody.[10][9]

A separate treasure account must not be silently merged with the Kakovatos report. Evans’s 1925 work describes gold rings and metal bead-seals found in 1915 in a rock-cut chamber tomb near Dombrena, by Thisbê in Boeotia, reportedly discovered by a peasant under wartime conditions.[9] That Thisbê/Dombrena assemblage is geographically and contextually distinct from the alleged Kakovatos find, and the supplied material does not identify a specific object from it as CMS VI, no. 277.[9]

## Description and iconography

The Ring of Nestor is identified in the supplied scholarly discussion as a gold signet ring and as CMS VI, no. 277.[6] The available evidence supports a broad description of a gold ring with a decorated bezel, but it does not provide authoritative measurements, a complete catalogue-style account of the hoop and bezel, or a specified manufacturing or surface-decoration technique for no. 277.[6][10][12] A measurement of 28 × 17 mm appears in a cited note, but the antecedent of the pronoun is unavailable; assigning those dimensions to the Ring of Nestor would therefore risk confusing it with another object.[9]

The imagery is preserved primarily through interpretation. Arthur Evans read the bezel as a Minoan Elysian or afterlife domain and described a human couple entering the Elysian Fields.[10] A reference note in the supplied material associates the ring with a seated goddess and a companion, but the referenced figure and surrounding description are not available in full.[9] A lower-credibility modern account calls the bezel depiction elaborate and characterizes it as an underworld scene; because that account does not provide a detailed image analysis or named authorities, the description should be treated as a secondary summary rather than a definitive physical description.[8]

A later iconographic proposal by A. G. Vlachopoulos, an archaeologist whose work concerns Aegean iconography, identifies the motif previously called Evans’s “watch-dog” or “dragon” as a scorpion. In that interpretation the scorpion is an apotropaic symbol that kills the “Minoan Dragon” of the ring.[6] The accessible abstract does not include the image analysis supporting the identification, so the scorpion reading remains an attributed interpretation rather than an uncontested observation. The evidence likewise does not establish whether the bezel design should technically be called engraved, carved, raised, or relief work.[6][10]

The ring should also be distinguished from other gold signet rings. The four gold rings from the Griffin Warrior’s grave near Pylos, for example, are a separate group and are not identified in the supplied material as the Ring of Nestor.[8] Similarly, the references to Evans’s drawings, watercolours, and replicas, and to the separately named Ring of Minos, do not establish that those representations or objects are the original CMS VI no. 277.[12]

## Authenticity debate

The authenticity debate began with conflicting assessments of the object’s status. The supplied historical account states that an archaeological board in Athens refused to buy the ring from its owner because its members considered it a forgery manufactured by a modern artist.[10] The same account says that Arthur Evans nevertheless privately purchased the object and accepted it as genuine, publishing it in 1925 and interpreting its bezel as a Minoan afterlife scene.[10] The board’s written report, examination procedure, and full list of participants are not supplied, so its judgment can be reported but not reconstructed in technical detail.

The principal objection concerns the absence of secure archaeological provenance. The category *gemma dubitanda* is used for sealstones and signet rings of alleged Bronze Age origin whose provenance and production date are unknown or unverified.[10] The supplied study states that such objects do not come from professional, well-documented excavations and that no decisive provenance reference was available for them.[10] Applied to the Ring of Nestor, this explains why the alleged Kakovatos findspot cannot by itself settle authenticity.

Arguments favoring authenticity have been renewed in later scholarship. A. G. Vlachopoulos’s 2020 contribution identifies the object as the so-called Ring of Nestor, CMS VI, no. 277, and reports that earlier identifications of certain Cretan–Mycenaean gold rings as fakes were reassessed in favor of authenticity.[6] The abstract attributes that reassessment to comparisons with wall paintings, gold rings, figurines, and ivories, together with closer study of the techniques and craftsmanship of signet rings dated to the first centuries of the Late Bronze Age, the 16th–15th centuries BC.[6] The captured abstract does not specify the individual comparative objects, laboratory procedures, or object-level results, so it records the direction of the argument without supplying its complete evidentiary demonstration.

The iconographic debate forms part of the same reassessment. Vlachopoulos identifies Evans’s “watch-dog” or “dragon” as a scorpion and interprets it as an apotropaic figure that kills the “Minoan Dragon.”[6] Conversely, an abstract concerning Egyptian iconography reports arguments that the ring’s semantic coherence depends on Egyptian prototypes and that its butterfly motif counts against authenticity.[7] Those arguments are available only in summary form in the supplied material and cannot be independently weighed here against the full comparative analysis.

The evidence therefore supports a historical conclusion rather than a definitive verdict. The ring has been accepted as authentic by Evans, rejected as a modern forgery in the reported Athenian assessment, and defended in a later reassessment based on iconographic and craftsmanship comparisons.[10][6] A 2011 review of CMS VI nevertheless explicitly states that doubts about the authenticity of no. 277 remained, even while other previously suspect pieces were rehabilitated.[12] The Ring of Nestor consequently remains a contested object whose cataloguing identity and interpretive importance are clearer than its archaeological provenance or final authenticity status.
