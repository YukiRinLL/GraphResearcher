Good Boy was written and directed by Ben Leonberg, who developed the film around the conceit of telling a haunted house story entirely from a dogs point of view.[2][3][7][8][11][15][16][19] Leonberg has discussed the project as a long-term effort, describing in interviews and profile pieces how a small core team worked over several years to realize the technically demanding concept.[15][19] According to a director profile, the film emerged from a multi-year collaboration in which a compact crew followed the dog performer through elaborate practical setups, emphasizing in-camera techniques rather than heavy reliance on visual effects.[7][15][17][19]

Cinematography for Good Boy is credited in public databases to Wade Grebnoel, a name that some sources indicate functions as a pseudonym associated with Ben Leonberg himself.[16][18] Other listings simply present Wade Grebnoel as the director of photography without noting any connection to Leonberg, leaving the precise relationship between the credited cinematographer and the director somewhat opaque.[16][18] Promotional materials for the film highlight the use of low-angle framing, handheld movement, and carefully choreographed blocking to simulate the way a dog would visually experience the haunted environment.[2][3][8][9][11]

Music for the film is attributed across sources to composer Sam Boase-Miller, with some materials also crediting Jeanmarie Riccobono.[16][18][21] A professional profile for Boase-Miller lists Good Boy among projects on which he is credited as a composer, and indicates that the film involved multiple credited composers.[21] This aligns with database entries that list Boase-Miller and Riccobono together as responsible for the score, even though certain summaries refer only to Boase-Miller, creating a minor discrepancy in how the music team is described.[16][18][21]

Good Boy was produced and distributed in partnership with IFC Films, with the independent label positioned prominently in marketing and release materials.[4][5][8][9][11][12] The production makes extensive use of a real dog performer, Indy/Indie, whose training and on-set work are discussed in featurettes and interviews that emphasize the challenges of eliciting specific behaviors on cue while maintaining narrative continuity.[7][17][19] Behind-the-scenes coverage highlights the intimate scale of the shoot, with accounts describing a compact core crew that contrasts with the longer formal credit lists found in public databases.[16][18][19]
