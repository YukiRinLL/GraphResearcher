Arthur Evans’s primary account of the Ring of Nestor situates it within a hoard of gold rings and bead‑seals that he described as "a sepulchral treasure" from a Mycenaean rock‑cut chamber tomb near the village of Dombrena, by Thisbê in Boeotia.[1][6] According to the summary on the Journal of Hellenic Studies website and Evans’s own monograph, the tomb and its contents were discovered in 1915 during wartime by a local peasant rather than through an organised excavation, and the assemblage included gold signet rings and bead‑seals together with bronze spearheads, short swords, razors, iron double‑axes and glass‑paste decorations.[1][6] Evans emphasised the presence of a short sword of "very late Mycenaean" type, an advanced bronze razor and a small perforated iron double‑axe head, as well as perforated glass‑paste elements bearing pendant gold disks, and used these associated objects to argue for a relatively late date within the Mycenaean period for the burial.[1][6]

Evans introduced the Ring of Nestor as the most elaborate of the gold signet rings from this treasure and characterised the hoard as "a truly royal treasure" of gold rings and bead‑seals, implying that the tomb belonged to a high‑status individual.[1] His article "‘The Ring of Nestor’: A Glimpse into the Minoan After‑World and A Sepulchral Treasure of Gold Signet‑Rings and Bead‑Seals from Thisbê, Boeotia" appeared in volume 45 of the Journal of Hellenic Studies in 1925 and was later republished in monograph form by Macmillan and Company.[1][6][7] The Cambridge journal entry identifies the article as authored by Sir Arthur Evans, with copyright attributed to the Society for the Promotion of Hellenic Studies and an online publication date of 23 December 2013, while the Getty Research Institute catalog and Google Books list the separate volume under Evans’s name with the same title and 1925 publication year.[1][7][12]

In his study Evans explained that the ring’s modern name was deliberately chosen as "The Ring of Nestor" to evoke the Homeric king of Pylos and to suggest that such a richly decorated gold signet might be imagined as belonging to a figure of that rank.[1][6] He did not claim a find‑spot at Pylos, and his title explicitly associates the treasure with Thisbê in Boeotia; the connection to Nestor is rhetorical and rooted in classical literature.[1][6][12] Evans’s interpretation of the engraved imagery as a "glimpse into the Minoan after‑world" and his emphasis on Cretan cult objects and tree worship embedded the Ring of Nestor in his broader reconstruction of Minoan religion and its diffusion into Mycenaean contexts.[6]

Later institutional and popular descriptions have repeated some elements of Evans’s discovery narrative while altering or simplifying others. The Ashmolean Museum’s publication on Arthur Evans and the Palace of Minos notes that the Ring of Nestor was first published by Evans, classifies it as a Late Minoan gold signet ring and states that it is now part of the Ashmolean collections.[3] Online posts and image‑based captions describe the ring as a Late Minoan gold signet from Crete, dated broadly to c. 1700–1450 BCE and sometimes linked to Kakovatos, while still acknowledging Evans’s 1925 publication and his interpretation of the scene as involving humans and mythical creatures in religious or underworld contexts.[5][16][18] These later accounts thus combine Evans’s emphasis on Minoan religious imagery with a more generic Cretan provenance assignment, in contrast to Evans’s original description of a find‑spot near Thisbê in Boeotia.[1][5][6][16][18]
