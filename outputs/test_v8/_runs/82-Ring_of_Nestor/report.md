# Ring of Nestor

The **Ring of Nestor** is a name associated with a gold signet ring, or with gold signet rings, in connection with the Mycenaean site of Pylos in Messenia. Jack L. Davis’s 2016 study, *The Lord of the Gold Rings*, concerns four gold signet rings bearing Minoan ritual scenes that were found in a Late Helladic IIA tomb near the Palace of Nestor, but it does not identify any of them as the Ring of Nestor.[3] An exhibition listing separately describes a Minoan gold “Signet Ring with a Ritual Scene,” dated 1630–1440 BCE and assigned to the Archaeological Museum of Chora; that listing also does not use the name Ring of Nestor.[5]

The Palace of Nestor at Ano Englianos was built shortly after 1300 BCE as an administrative centre in Messenia and was destroyed by a major fire around 1200 BCE.[2] The documented records therefore establish a Pylos archaeological context for related gold signet rings, but they do not establish that the four rings from the Griffin Warrior tomb and the Chora exhibition object are the same object, or provide a formal catalogue identification for a ring called the Ring of Nestor.[3][5]

## Description and archaeological context

## Description and archaeological context

Davis’s 2016 study records four gold signet rings bearing Minoan ritual scenes among the grave goods of a large stone-built tomb discovered in May 2015 near Tholos Tomb IV at the Palace of Nestor, Pylos. The study’s abstract dates the tomb to the Late Helladic IIA period. It identifies the single male occupant as approximately 30–35 years old and uses the designation “Griffin Warrior”; hundreds of objects made of gold, silver, bronze, ivory, and semiprecious stone accompanied the burial.[3]

The study identifies the four gold objects as signet rings decorated with Minoan ritual scenes.[3] Its abstract and the publication information reproduced with it do not give the individual inventory numbers or state that any one of the four rings bears the name Ring of Nestor.[3] The tomb’s date and excavation context consequently describe those four documented rings and should not be assigned to another ring without an explicit identification.

An exhibition preview published by *Art of The Ancestors* lists a gold “Signet Ring with a Ritual Scene,” attributes it to the Minoan tradition, dates it to 1630–1440 BCE, and assigns it to the Archaeological Museum of Chora under museum number SN24-0030 and exhibition number VEX.2025.1.81.[5] The preview does not state the object’s findspot, excavation context, ownership history, or scholarly publication, and it does not call the object the Ring of Nestor.[5] These details are therefore exhibition-listing information rather than independently corroborated facts about the identity or provenance of the Ring of Nestor.

The Palace of Nestor at Ano Englianos provides the wider archaeological setting. The National Archaeological Museum describes the palace as an administrative centre of a kingdom controlling almost the whole of Messenia, built shortly after 1300 BCE and destroyed by a major fire around 1200 BCE.[2] Those dates concern the palace and site history; they do not date or establish the provenance of either the four Griffin Warrior rings or the Chora-listed object.[2][3][5]

## Interpretation and reception

## Iconography of related signet rings

Davis’s study describes the four signet rings from the Griffin Warrior tomb as bearing Minoan ritual scenes.[3] A caption for one figure describes a male bull-leaper dismounting in mid-air behind a bull. It also describes the bull’s tail crossing in front of the leaper’s left arm and records details of the bull and the leaper’s clothing and hair.[3] The supplied description identifies the imagery on one of the four rings studied by Davis; it does not identify that ring as the Ring of Nestor.[3]

The Pylos connection is documented through the archaeological setting. The National Archaeological Museum presents the Palace of Nestor at Ano Englianos as a Messenian administrative centre built shortly after 1300 BCE and destroyed around 1200 BCE.[2] Davis’s study places the four signet rings in a tomb near that palace, while the Chora exhibition preview lists a separate Minoan signet ring with a ritual scene.[2][3][5]

The two records should be kept distinct. Davis’s publication concerns four rings from a specified tomb and reports their Minoan ritual imagery; the Chora preview gives a date, material, museum number, and exhibition number for another listed ring, but does not link it to the tomb or use the name Ring of Nestor.[3][5] On the cited descriptions, the bull-leaping imagery can be attributed to one of the Griffin Warrior tomb rings, while the identity of an object formally called the Ring of Nestor is not established by these records.[3][5]
