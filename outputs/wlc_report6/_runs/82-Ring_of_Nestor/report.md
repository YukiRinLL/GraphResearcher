# Research Report

## “Ring of Nestor” archaeological artifact discovery origin dating description

- The Ring of Nestor is described as a large Aegean gold signet ring measuring approximately 3.29 cm by 2.18 cm and is noted as the fifth-largest Aegean signet ring. [1]

- Vlachopoulos reviews earlier claims that the Ring of Nestor was a forgery and concludes, on the basis of new research combining iconographic and technical analysis, that the ring is authentic. [1]

- The chapter confirms that the Ring of Nestor belongs to the Ashmolean Museum in Oxford and discusses its display and scholarly reception within that institution. [1]

- Eliopoulos examines the ring’s bezel scenes, including altars, cult scenes and possibly ships and worshippers, and compares them with other Aegean seals as part of his iconographic analysis of authenticity. [2]

- The article states that contradictions in prior technical analyses of the Ring of Nestor highlight the importance of iconography as a tool for evaluating such pieces. [2]

- Eliopoulos argues that the connection between incurved altars depicted on the ring and similar features in Aegean art supports the ring’s authenticity. [2]

- According to the ResearchGate article, "In 1995 the ring was once again placed on display in the Ashmolean Museum." [3]

- The article refers to "cult scenes illustrated in miniature on administrative stone seals and metal signet [rings]" in discussing the Ring of Nestor’s imagery. [3]

- Evans’s work is described as discussing "a sepulchral treasure of gold signet-rings and bead-seals from Thisbê, Boeotia," which includes the Ring of Nestor. [4]

- Evans interprets the ring’s engraved scenes as providing "a glimpse into the Minoan after-world" and links them to a voyage and heroic cult within a beehive tomb context. [4]

- In The Palace of Minos, Evans notes the "beaded gold hoop resembling that of 'Ring of Nestor'" when describing the discovery of a royal signet ring and comments on the parallel arrangement of three successive scenes. [5]

- The Brill chapter remarks that "The gold 'Ring of Nestor' and 'Ring of Minos', which both surfaced in the 1920s, [are] famous examples, generally believed to be fake." [6]

- The blog cites "For the Ring of Nestor, see Evans 1925, p. 47, fig. 42" and asserts that "Ring 2 would seem to support the authenticity of both rings even in small details." [7]

- The Academia article notes that Evans 1925 described a "dark film over the gold surface" of the Ring of Nestor as decoration and uses this observation in its technical discussion. [8]

- The Ashmolean Museum image library entry for AN1938.1129 reads: "Gold signet ring with design of male and female couple facing ship - Middle Minoan III Period - Late Minoan I Period (c. 1700 - c. 1450 BC)." [9]

- Hudler states that "Gemmae dubitandae, such as the 'Ring of Nestor', illustrate" the problems that arise when the archaeological properties of objects are uncertain or contested. [10]

- The Scribd document refers to the ring’s "discovery" and reports that it was said to have been found in a "large beehive tomb at Nestor," before questioning this narrative. [11]

- The same Scribd text notes that "the ring of Nestor appeared and it is worth stressing that 'only Gilléron could …'" in relation to its creation, implying possible involvement of Émile Gilléron. [11]

- Berry writes of a "design that bears a clear resemblance to the 'Ring of Nestor,' even featuring those otherwise unattested chrysalises" and questions whether such resemblance is proof of forgery or authenticity. [12]

- A Facebook post describes the "Ring of Nestor – A ~3700-year-old gold signet ring from Crete, Late Minoan Period, (c. 1700-1450 BCE), now housed at the Ashmolean Museum in Oxford." [13]

## "Ring of Nestor" museum collection catalogue PDF

- The catalogue provides for each cast the identification, subject, date of the original, present location of the original, and the artist or school or period, and states in its preface that it aims to be 'a succinct handbook for students' focusing on scientific catalogues of collections. [14]

- In the section 'Aegean Prehistory (nos. 1–31)', each entry lists an inventory number (e.g., CAM 30, PP 170), provenance such as Phylakopi on Melos, a chronological designation like 'Late Cycladic I' or 'Middle Cycladic III – Late Cycladic I', and a brief object type description such as 'bathtub fragment' or 'bowl with potter’s marks'. [15]

- The IGNCA PDF includes the numbered headings '(2) The Ring of Nestor' and 'V. Restoration of the design on the Ring of Nestor in the style of the Miniature Frescoes of Knossos', along with mention of a 'Signet-ring from Thisbe'. [16]

- The Nestor 2020 issue contains bibliographic listings and conference abstracts that reference work on the Ring of Nestor, including articles on its iconography and authenticity in journals such as the Oxford Journal of Archaeology and chapters in edited volumes on Aegean iconography. [17]

- The Hellenic Studies article describes 'a royal treasure of gold rings and bead-seals' that was 'found in 1915 by a peasant in a chamber tomb near the village of Dombrena' at a Mycenaean rock tomb near the site of Thisbê in Boeotia. [18]

- The author notes that 'it is impossible without a breach of confidence to give all details of the find circumstances', and explains that 'war conditions' affected the normal archaeological reporting of the discovery. [18]

- Among other objects from the same group of tombs, the article mentions bronze spear-heads and a short sword of late Mycenaean type. [18]

- Vlachopoulos refers to the Ring of Nestor as 'CMS VI, no. 277' and discusses its place in the Corpus der minoischen und mykenischen Siegel as part of the glyptic corpus. [1]

- The chapter reports that 'The Ring of Nestor measures 3.29 cm by 2.18 cm and is the fifth largest Aegean signet ring', providing precise dimensions and a comparative size ranking. [1]

- Vlachopoulos dates the Ring of Nestor to 'the first centuries of the Late Bronze Age (16th–15th century BC)', correlating its style and iconography with material from that period. [1]

- The chapter states that the central figure previously termed Evans’s 'watch-dog' or 'dragon' should be identified as a scorpion, and interprets this scorpion as a 'major apotropaic symbol' that 'kills the “Minoan Dragon”', offering a key to the composition’s meaning. [1]

- Vlachopoulos argues that 'subsequent finds of comparable iconographic subjects (wall-paintings, gold rings, figurines, ivories, etc.) and detailed study of techniques and craftsmanship have shifted scholarly opinion decisively in favor of authenticity' of the Ring of Nestor. [1]

- Eliopoulos describes the Ring of Nestor as being at the center of a long-standing debate over the authenticity of certain Creto-Mycenaean gold rings, alongside the Ring of Minos, with some scholars having proposed that these rings are modern creations. [2]

- The article argues that 'iconographic analysis is of equal importance to technical examination' for assessing the authenticity of the Ring of Nestor, stressing that motifs on the ring must be compared with those on securely provenanced Aegean artifacts. [2]

- Eliopoulos points to the depiction of incurved altars on the Ring of Nestor and connects them to similar altar motifs in Aegean iconography, claiming that this correspondence supports the ring’s authenticity. [2]

- Eliopoulos notes that interpretations of the ring’s imagery have sometimes reflected cultural practices and perspectives from the 1920s and later, implying that modern viewpoints have shaped the debate over its iconography and authenticity. [2]

- Pini’s article is framed as a response to arguments that the Ring of Nestor was a modern forgery by the Gilliérons, and it presents 'new technical observations' intended to support the ring’s authenticity. [19]

- The article states that the Ring of Nestor 'showcases unique stylistic and manufacturing details that align with known Minoan-Mycenaean artifacts, reinforcing its ancient origin', based on comparison with securely provenanced objects. [19]
