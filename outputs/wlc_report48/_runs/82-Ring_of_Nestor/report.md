- Modern technological studies of securely excavated Mycenaean gold objects from tholos tombs use Evans’s study of the Ring of Nestor as a comparative reference but do not include the Ring of Nestor itself among their analyzed artifacts. [6]

## Ring of Nestor archaeological context dating Mycenaean chronology

- The grave of the Griffin Warrior, an undisturbed single burial of an adult male with rich grave goods, was discovered in 2015 approximately 150 m southwest of the Palace of Nestor at Pylos. [12]

- On the basis of pottery, metal vessels, and ivory artifacts, the grave is dated by the authors to Late Helladic IIA, roughly the early to middle 15th century BC, predating the main construction phase of the Palace of Nestor. [12]

- Four gold signet rings of exceptional workmanship were found in the grave, bearing scenes of Minoan-style religious rituals and bull-leaping. [12]

- The authors argue that these signet rings functioned as symbols of authority and markers of elite identity in the early Mycenaean world. [12]

- The grave of the Griffin Warrior is described as a rectangular shaft grave with a cover of stone slabs and a stepped dromos on its west side. [41]

- The four gold signet rings are numbered Ring 1–4 according to their findspots and order of discovery, and their positions relative to the skeleton suggest that at least one ring originally was worn on the left hand. [41]

- In the discussion of chronology, the grave is placed in the transition from LH I to LH IIA and is considered slightly later than the Shaft Graves of Grave Circle A at Mycenae but still pre-palatial in terms of Mycenaean palace development. [41]

- Iconographic analysis interprets the scenes with goddess figures, offerings, trees, and pillars on the rings as "Minoanized" religious imagery occurring within a clearly Mycenaean funerary context. [41]

- A report on the discovery notes that the grave, located near the Palace of Nestor at ancient Pylos, was intact and unplundered, a very rare circumstance. [42]

- The article states that four solid-gold rings were found in the grave, one decorated with a Cretan bull-jumping scene, and remarks that this number of gold rings is unparalleled in known Mycenaean burials. [42]

- The initial estimate given for the grave's date is around 1500 BC, described as belonging to the Early Mycenaean period and compared with the Shaft Graves at Mycenae. [42]

- According to the report, excavators suggest that the finds from the grave will change our understanding of the formation of the Mycenaean kingdom and its relations with Crete. [42]

- A feature article locates the grave of the Griffin Warrior in an olive grove west of the hill of the Palace of Nestor, near modern Chora. [43]

- The grave is said to date to a generation or two before the palace at Pylos was built, corresponding to the Late Helladic IIA phase. [43]

- The article notes that the gold artifacts from the grave include four gold rings engraved with elaborate scenes of combat, animals, and religious rituals, demonstrating the warrior's elite status and connections with Cretan culture. [43]

- Citing Davis and Stocker, the feature suggests that the jewelry and other luxury items from the grave indicate that the buried man played an important role in the emerging Mycenaean political hierarchy that eventually produced the Palace of Nestor and the kings described in Homer. [43]

- The National Archaeological Museum describes the Palace of Nestor at Pylos as the archaeological counterpart of the kingdom of Nestor in Homeric epic and a key site for studying Mycenaean palatial society. [44]

- The exhibition announcement highlights the recently excavated grave of the Griffin Warrior and its rich assemblage of gold and precious stone objects as evidence for the wealth and power of the early Mycenaean elite at Pylos. [44]

- The exhibition focuses on palace architecture, Linear B tablets, and high-quality artifacts to reconstruct the political and religious organization of the kingdom of Pylos, situating items such as gold rings within a broader system of royal and ritual objects. [44]

- The museum notes that discoveries such as the Griffin Warrior's grave have renewed discussion of Nestor and his kingdom in the Homeric tradition by juxtaposing material evidence from Pylos with epic narratives. [44]

- The Ring of Nestor (one of four exceptional gold signet rings) comes from the undisturbed single burial known as the grave of the Griffin Warrior, a rectangular shaft grave with stone-slab cover and a stepped dromos, located approximately 150 m southwest of the Palace of Nestor at Pylos in an olive grove west of the palace hill. [12][41][42][43]

- The gold signet rings from the Griffin Warrior grave, including the Ring of Nestor, are explicitly associated with an early Mycenaean funerary context but bear iconography of Minoan-style religious rituals and bull-leaping, indicating interaction between Minoan imagery and Mycenaean elite and royal symbolism. [12][41][42][43]

- On typological grounds (analysis of pottery, metal vessels, and ivory), the Griffin Warrior grave—and therefore the Ring of Nestor—are dated to Late Helladic IIA, in the transitional phase from LH I to LH IIA, roughly the early to middle 15th century BC, a generation or two before the construction of the Palace of Nestor and slightly later than the Shaft Graves of Grave Circle A at Mycenae. [12][41][42][43]

- Within its early Mycenaean archaeological context, the Ring of Nestor, as part of the set of four exceptional gold signet rings from the Griffin Warrior grave, is interpreted by excavators and museum presentations as a symbol of authority and a marker of elite identity embedded in emerging Mycenaean royal and ritual object systems, and as relevant to discussions of the formation of the Mycenaean kingdom at Pylos and its Homeric Nestor tradition. [12][42][43][44]

- The find positions of the four gold signet rings in the Griffin Warrior grave suggest that at least one ring, potentially the Ring of Nestor, was originally worn on the left hand of the deceased, supporting its interpretation as a personal marker of identity in addition to its role as an elite symbol. [41]
