# Research Report

## “Ring of Nestor” archaeological gold ring Pylos discovery excavations

- Evans introduces a "sepulchral treasure of gold signet-rings and bead-seals from Thisbê, Boeotia" and identifies among them a large gold signet-ring that he names the "Ring of Nestor," describing it as the most important piece of the find. [1]

- The article states that the treasure, including the Ring of Nestor, was discovered in 1915 in a Mycenaean rock-cut chamber tomb near Thisbê by a local peasant at a place called Dombrena, during wartime conditions that prevented normal archaeological oversight. [1]

- Evans describes the Ring of Nestor as a large gold signet-ring with a bezel bearing elaborate figured scenes, and he interprets these scenes as providing a "glimpse into the Minoan after-world." [1]

- In situating the ring chronologically, Evans argues that it belongs to Late Helladic or Late Mycenaean times but displays pronounced Minoan stylistic elements, particularly in its glyptic style and religious imagery. [1]

- The JHS article describes the find in the rock tomb as a "truly royal treasure" and notes that the gold rings and bead seals, including the Ring of Nestor, passed through private hands before Evans was able to examine and document them. [2]

- Evans provides a technical description of the Ring of Nestor as constructed from gold sheet, with a large bezel attached to a hoop, and describes multiple scenes on the bezel featuring anthropomorphic figures, animals, architectural structures, and landscape or celestial elements. [2]

- The Heidelberg edition’s table of contents lists section I as "Sepulchral treasure of gold signet-rings and bead-seals from Thisbê, Boeotia" and section II as "A glimpse into the Minoan after-world," followed by plates I–V illustrating the Ring of Nestor and related objects. [3]

- Within the first section, Evans catalogs the rings and bead seals from the Thisbe tomb, giving physical measurements and manufacturing notes, and identifies the Ring of Nestor as distinguished by its unusually large size and intricate glyptic decoration compared to the other pieces. [3]

- The second section of Evans’s study interprets the ring’s imagery as showing processional and ritual scenes with possible deities and worshippers, alongside symbolic motifs such as trees, altars, and celestial elements, which he takes to reflect conceptions of the afterlife. [3]

- The HathiTrust record specifies the title "ʻThe ring of Nestor;̓ a glimpse into the Minoan after-world, and a sepulchral treasure of gold signet-rings and bead-seals from Thisbê, Boeotia" and notes that the publication includes five plates. [4]

- J.L. Davis writes that before the Griffin Warrior discovery, the best-known large, elaborately engraved gold signet ring associated with the Pylos area was the "Ring of Nestor," which had long served as a key comparative piece in discussions of Mycenaean gold rings. [5]

- The article notes that Arthur Evans acquired the Ring of Nestor from local sources rather than as part of a controlled excavation, and states that as a result its archaeological credentials and precise provenance are debated among scholars. [5]

- Davis indicates that the Ring of Nestor may not have originated from the Palace of Nestor excavations themselves, suggesting that earlier assumptions linking it directly to that site should be treated with caution. [5]

- In "The Lord of the Gold Rings," Davis et al. describe the Ring of Nestor as a celebrated gold signet ring with elaborate iconography and remark that its band and bezel are technically and stylistically comparable to rings from Tholos Tomb IV at Pylos and to the Ring of Minos. [6]

- The authors use these comparisons to argue for placing the Ring of Nestor within a particular phase of Mycenaean glyptic development and to suggest that it may share workshop traditions with rings from securely excavated Pylian tombs. [6]

- Davis and colleagues state that because the Ring of Nestor lacks secure excavation context and has a problematic acquisition history, it should be treated with caution and not relied upon as primary evidence for detailed reconstructions of Pylian archaeological contexts. [6]

- The related Academia.edu version of the study notes that "Both the Ring of Nestor and the Ring of Minos have bands strikingly similar" to certain rings from excavations at the Palace of Nestor, Pylos. [7]

- This version emphasizes that objects like the Ring of Nestor, which were acquired under early 20th-century conditions without secure documentation, must be integrated into archaeological syntheses only with explicit acknowledgment of their uncertain provenance. [7]

- Cathrin Hudler cites the Ring of Nestor as an example of a "gemma dubitanda" because its archaeological properties are uncertain, noting that it entered collections without secure excavation records and that its context, and thus its evidentiary value, remain unclear. [8]

- Hudler argues that reliance on the Ring of Nestor in arguments about Mycenaean religious beliefs, chronology, or social practices can "escape" methodological rigor because the absence of secure context makes such interpretations difficult to verify. [8]

- Hudler recommends that scholars explicitly label objects like the Ring of Nestor as problematic and limit their role in reconstructions of specific historical or ritual scenarios, suggesting instead that they be used cautiously and primarily in broader stylistic or technological discussions. [8]

- The technological study of gold objects from four tholos tombs at Pylos lists Arthur Evans’s "The Ring of Nestor: A glimpse into the Minoan after-world" and J.L. Davis et al.’s "The Lord of the Gold Rings: The Griffin Warrior of Pylos" among its references when discussing Mycenaean gold rings. [9]

- In comparing production techniques and alloy compositions of gold rings from Pylian tholos tombs to famous examples, the authors mention the Ring of Nestor alongside the Griffin Warrior rings, using them to discuss possible shared workshop traditions and technological parallels while noting the Ring of Nestor’s uncertain provenance. [9]

- The technological study explicitly cautions that, because the Ring of Nestor does not come from a securely excavated context, it should not be used on an equal footing with firmly provenanced Pylian finds when drawing conclusions about local production and deposition practices. [9]

- The Ring of Nestor is a large gold signet ring, constructed from gold sheet with a hoop and a large bezel bearing multiple elaborate figured scenes, including anthropomorphic figures, animals, architectural structures, and natural or celestial motifs. [1][2][3]

- Arthur J. Evans introduced and named the Ring of Nestor as the most important piece of a sepulchral treasure of gold signet rings and bead seals reportedly found in a Mycenaean rock-cut chamber tomb near Thisbê, in Boeotia. [1][2]

- According to Arthur Evans’s account, the treasure that included the Ring of Nestor was discovered in 1915 in a Mycenaean rock-cut tomb near Thisbê at a place called Dombrena by a local peasant, and the objects passed through private hands before Evans examined them. [1][2]

- The Ring of Nestor lacks secure excavation records and was acquired by Arthur Evans from local sources rather than through controlled archaeological excavation, leaving its precise provenance and archaeological credentials debated and uncertain. [5][6][7][8][9]

- Modern scholars such as Cathrin Hudler and J.L. Davis classify the Ring of Nestor as a problematic or "gemma dubitanda" object whose unclear provenance undermines its reliability for precise arguments about Mycenaean chronology, function, religious beliefs, or specific Pylian archaeological contexts. [6][8][9]

- Despite its uncertain provenance, the Ring of Nestor is widely treated in scholarship as a celebrated masterpiece of Aegean gold glyptic and has long played a prominent comparative role in studies of Mycenaean gold rings. [5][9]

- Arthur Evans dates the Ring of Nestor to the Late Helladic or Late Mycenaean period and argues that its glyptic style and religious imagery show strong Minoan stylistic influence. [1][2]

- Evans interprets the Ring of Nestor’s iconography—processional and ritual scenes with possible deities and worshippers, trees, altars, and celestial motifs—as offering a vision or "glimpse" of a Minoan-style afterworld and reflecting beliefs about the afterlife. [1][2][3][4]

- Comparative stylistic and technical studies by Davis and colleagues note that the band and bezel of the Ring of Nestor are strikingly similar to rings from Tholos Tomb IV at Pylos and to the so-called Ring of Minos, and use these similarities to suggest a particular phase of Mycenaean glyptic development and possible shared workshop traditions. [6][7][9]

- Recent discussions question earlier assumptions that the Ring of Nestor originated from the Palace of Nestor excavations at Pylos, emphasizing that it may not come from that site and therefore should not be equated with securely excavated Pylian assemblages. [5][9]

- The object known as the Ring of Nestor is a gold signet ring, reportedly found in 1924 in a tomb assemblage at Kakovatos in Messenia/Thisbê–Boeotia context, now in the Ashmolean Museum, Oxford, under inventory number AN1938.1130 (also cited as 1938–1130). [4][10][13][16]

- The Ring of Nestor is described in modern scholarship and museum catalogues as a gold signet ring of Late Minoan I–IB–II date, with proposed absolute dates ranging approximately from 1600 to 1400 BC. [10][15][16]

- Technical descriptions in the Cambridge 'Minoan Glyptic' chapter record that the Ring of Nestor is a gold signet ring measuring approximately 3.27 cm in length. [16]

- Iconographic descriptions report that the Ring of Nestor’s bezel scene includes 14 human figures and several fantastic creatures, making it one of the most densely populated multi-figure compositions known among Aegean signet rings. [11]

- Scholarly and museum descriptions treat the Ring of Nestor’s imagery as 'pseudo-Minoan' and emphasize Egyptian and hybrid iconographic elements, including motifs such as incurved altars that have been used in debates over the ring’s authenticity. [12][13]

- The Ring of Nestor has been the subject of a long-standing authenticity debate: Arthur J. Evans accepted it as genuine, but many scholars condemned it as a forgery; despite persistent doubts noted in later reviews, the ring is nonetheless included as no. 277 in the Corpus der minoischen und mykenischen Siegel. [11][12][13][14]
