Evans’s 1925 study provides the most detailed technical description of the Ring of Nestor, identifying it as a substantial gold signet ring and giving its dimensions, weight and construction details.[6] He describes the ring as having a carefully formed hoop and an enlarged, slightly curved bezel suitable for engraving a complex scene, and illustrates it with plates showing photographs and line drawings of the ring and its seal impressions.[1][6] Later catalogue‑style descriptions and image captions classify the piece as a Late Minoan gold signet ring dated approximately to 1700–1450 BCE, and one such caption identifies it as a "Minoan Kakovatos signet ring" with a weight of 31.8 (likely grams), now in the Ashmolean Museum in Oxford.[3][16][18] The Ashmolean publication on Arthur Evans confirms that the ring is part of the museum’s collections and highlights its status as a major example of Evans’s acquisitions of Late Minoan glyptic.[3]

The bezel of the Ring of Nestor carries a densely composed scene divided into several segments, and both Evans and later commentators emphasize the multiplicity of figures and motifs.[1][6][18] Evans’s iconographic description notes multiple human figures engaged in ritual activities, a prominent tree, curved altars and other architectural or cultic structures, as well as animals and hybrid creatures.[1][6] Descriptive and popular accounts similarly refer to "humans and mythical creatures" arranged in a continuous narrative, and one modern caption mentions "four cult scenes" on the ring.[16][18] A Reddit commentary on the ring’s image states that the composition consists of a vertical column at the left of the seal impression and two scenes of encounters between people, suggesting that viewers perceive the surface as organised into separate but related episodes.[17] Later iconographic analyses describe the Ring of Nestor as an extremely detailed Minoan‑style gold signet in which small motifs are arranged with great deliberation, and they compare aspects of the design with Egyptian iconography, focusing on how specific elements might reflect cross‑cultural influences.[13]

Evans interpreted elements such as the curved altar and the tree as "Cretan cult‑objects" and as evidence for tree worship, integrating them into his reconstruction of Minoan religious practice.[6] He characterised the overall composition as offering "a glimpse into the Minoan after‑world" and argued that it depicted religious or funerary scenes, possibly associated with an underworld realm.[1][6][12] Modern summaries echo this reading, stating that Evans saw the design as showing humans and mythical beings involved in religious scenes with possible underworld associations and connections between Minoan civilisation and ancient Egypt.[18] Subsequent iconographic work has treated the ring’s imagery as a key example of elaborate ritual scenes in Aegean glyptic and has examined how its numerous small motifs, including animals, cult objects and architectural forms, contribute to an integrated narrative design.[13][14]

Technical studies have focused not only on the ring’s iconography but also on its manufacture and condition. Ingo Pini’s analysis documents measurements such as the weight, thickness and dimensions of the hoop and bezel, and examines the goldworking techniques and wear patterns.[15] According to the summary of his article, Pini argues that the ring’s stylistic and manufacturing features correspond to those of securely provenanced Minoan–Mycenaean rings and seals, and that observed wear supports its interpretation as an ancient object rather than a modern fabrication.[15] At the same time, some modern discussions note that aspects of the iconography and execution have led scholars to suspect that the ring might be a modern forgery, possibly reflecting cultural practices of the 1920s, and link these suspicions to the figure of Émile Gilliéron fils, while others maintain that its physical characteristics are compatible with Bronze Age craftsmanship.[3][14][18]
