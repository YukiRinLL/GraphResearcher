## “Ring of Nestor” Mycenaean gold ring archaeology iconography

- The paper is explicitly titled as a study of the iconography of the “Ring of Nestor,” dealing with its imagery within the broader context of Aegean Bronze Age signet rings and, importantly, the issue of forgeries in this corpus. [1]

- The author reviews the ring’s iconographic program, referencing motifs such as female figures, possible deities, cult scenes, and religious symbols that are often associated with Minoan and Mycenaean glyptic art. [1]

- The paper compares the scenes on the Ring of Nestor with securely provenanced Minoan and Mycenaean rings, assessing whether the composition and style fit within known traditions or display anachronistic mixtures suggestive of a modern forgery. [1]

- Arthur Evans presents the “Ring of Nestor” as part of a “sepulchral treasure of gold signet-rings and bead-seals from Thisbê, Boeotia,” associating the ring with a burial context in Boeotia on the Greek mainland. [2]

- The monograph describes the ring as a massive gold signet ring with an elaborately engraved bezel depicting a complex scene interpreted by Evans as showing aspects of the “Minoan after-world.” [2]

- Evans argues that although the ring was found (or at least said to have been found) at Thisbê in Boeotia, the style and iconography of the engraved scene are essentially Minoan, suggesting connections between mainland Greece and Minoan Crete. [2]

- Evans situates the ring chronologically within the Late Minoan / Mycenaean period, using stylistic comparison with other Minoan and Mycenaean gold signet rings and seals. [2]

- The title describes the object as the “Pseudo-Minoan Nestor Ring,” indicating that the author questions its genuine Minoan origin or suggests it is an imitation or forgery constructed in a Minoan-like style. [3]

- The article focuses on “Egyptian iconography” present in the scenes engraved on the Nestor Ring, arguing that certain motifs derive from Egyptian visual culture rather than from authentic Aegean Bronze Age traditions. [3]

- The study compares the iconographic elements on the Nestor Ring—such as boats, deities, ritual scenes, offering tables or altars, sacred trees, or composite beings—with visual parallels in Egyptian art, suggesting that the ring’s imagery may reflect modern scholarly knowledge of Egyptian and Minoan motifs. [3]

- The author highlights that the Ring of Nestor combines several motifs—boats, female individuals, altars, sacred trees, baetyls—that are individually known in Aegean glyptic but rarely, if ever, combined in such a complex narrative scene, raising suspicion that the ring is a modern pastiche. [4]

- The chapter reviews the history of the ring’s discovery and acquisition, noting that the ring lacks firm archaeological documentation and that its supposed provenance from Thisbê, Boeotia, has been questioned. [4]

- The discussion situates the Nestor Ring within a broader pattern of 19th–20th century forgeries produced for the antiquities market, emphasizing the need for caution when interpreting unprovenanced “masterpieces” that conveniently illustrate contemporary scholarly theories. [4]

- The engraved scene on CMS I, 179 is interpreted as depicting a female deity, possibly Demeter, accompanied by an eagle associated with Zeus, suggesting a mythological or cultic context. [5]

- The author argues that the ring’s imagery symbolizes the eternal cycle of the seasons, linking the scene to themes of fertility, agricultural renewal, and the alternation of life and death. [5]

- It notes that the only previously known comparable gold signet ring from this area was the “famous Ring of Nestor,” which had been purchased by Arthur Evans, implying that the ring did not come from systematic excavation. [6]

- The text indicates that the Griffin Warrior finds, including their rings, are securely excavated and have clear archaeological context, in contrast to older finds like the Nestor Ring whose provenance and authenticity have been debated. [6]

- The bibliography lists I. Pini, “Minoan and Mycenaean Decorated Gold Finger-Rings,” Bulletin of the Institute of Classical Studies 42 (1997–1998) 210–211, and I. Pini, “The ‘Ring of Nestor’,” Oxford Journal of Archaeology 17 (1998) 1–13, among key references. [7]

## catalogue "Ring of Nestor" gold ring museum database

- The article describes the Ring of Nestor as part of "a sepulchral treasure of gold signet-rings and bead-seals from Thisbê, Boeotia," and presents it as a gold signet ring with imagery interpreted as offering a glimpse into the "Minoan after-world." [8]

- In discussing a signet ring in the City Art Museum of St. Louis, the article references "THE RING OF NESTOR. (EVANS, PM. IV, FIG. 915b)," indicating that Evans's illustration of the Ring of Nestor in Palace of Minos volume IV, figure 915b, is used as a comparison. [9]

- The AJA review remarks, "While some may still harbor doubts as to the authenticity of the ‘Ring of Nestor’ (no. 277)…", identifying the Ring of Nestor as catalogue number 277 in the CMS volume and explicitly noting ongoing doubts about its authenticity. [10]

- The IGNCA PDF lists a sequence including "(1) Signet-ring from Thisbe. (2) The Ring of Nestor. V. Restoration of the design on the Ring of Nestor in the style of the Miniature Frescoes of Knossos …", suggesting a plate or figure showing the original ring and a restored drawing modeled on Knossian miniature frescoes. [11]

- The Met Museum Perspectives essay refers to "gold signet rings known as the Ring of Minos and the Ring of Nestor, and the well-known disk from Phaistos on Crete… Gilliéron …", linking these named rings to Emile Gilliéron’s historic images and reconstructions of Greek Bronze Age artifacts. [12]

- Hudler's paper cites the classic JHS article "‘The Ring of Nestor’: A Glimpse into the Minoan After-World and A Sepulchral Treasure of Gold Signet-Rings and Bead-Seals from Thisbê, Boeotia" as a reference when discussing contested Aegean signet rings. [13]

- The article "Deconstructing a Narrative of Epiphany on the Isopata Ring" notes that the "ring of Nestor (Evans PM III, 145–57)" has been included among examples used to support narratives of divine epiphany, citing Evans’s discussion in Palace of Minos volume III, pages 145–157. [14]

- The Academia.edu paper is described as presenting "an analysis of the iconography surrounding the ‘Ring of Nestor’ in the context of Aegean forgeries and authenticity debates," emphasizing both the ring's imagery and its contested status. [1]

- The gold signet ring known as the 'Ring of Nestor' comes from a sepulchral treasure of gold signet-rings and bead-seals reportedly found at Thisbê (Thisbe) in Boeotia and has been interpreted as depicting imagery related to the Minoan afterworld. [8][11][13]

- The 'Ring of Nestor' is catalogued in the Corpus der minoischen und mykenischen Siegel, Volume 6 (Ashmolean Museum), as entry number 277 and is commonly cited in modern scholarship via that CMS reference. [10][1]

- Scholars continue to debate the authenticity of the 'Ring of Nestor'; while it is incorporated into catalogues such as CMS 6 (no. 277), some authors explicitly note ongoing doubts and treat it as a contested or potentially forged Aegean signet ring. [10][13][1]

- The 'Ring of Nestor' has been widely reproduced and reconstructed, notably through Emile Gilliéron’s historic images, and has been used in modern scholarship as a key comparative example in discussions of Minoan epiphany narratives and Aegean signet ring iconography. [11][12][14]

- Evans’s 'Palace of Minos' volumes provide key early published documentation of the 'Ring of Nestor', with the ring discussed in volume III (pp. 145–157) and illustrated in volume IV (fig. 915b), and these references are repeatedly cited in later scholarly treatments and comparative studies of Aegean gold signet rings. [9][14]
