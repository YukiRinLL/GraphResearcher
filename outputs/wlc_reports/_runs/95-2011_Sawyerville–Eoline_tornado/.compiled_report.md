# 2011 Sawyerville–Eoline tornado

## Lead

The **2011 Sawyerville–Eoline tornado** was a long‑tracked, strong tornado that occurred in central Alabama during the afternoon and early evening of April 27, 2011, as part of the 2011 Super Outbreak of tornadoes across the southeastern United States. The tornado was rated EF3 on the Enhanced Fujita scale by the National Weather Service (NWS), with an estimated maximum wind speed of about 145 mph (233 km/h), a maximum path width of roughly 1 mile (1,760 yd), and an overall path length of 72.13 miles (116.1 km) across Greene, Hale, and Bibb counties. Along its track from near Tishabee in far southwest Greene County through the communities of Sawyerville, Harper Hill, and Eoline to near Marvel in northeastern Bibb County, the tornado caused severe damage to hundreds of structures, including the destruction of numerous mobile homes, single‑family houses, a church, and the Eoline Fire Department, and it downed thousands of trees. NWS Birmingham’s event summary and outbreak table attribute 7 fatalities and 50 injuries to the full Sawyerville–Eoline tornado track, while the NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) Storm Events database segment for the Hale County portion lists 6 direct deaths and 40 direct injuries, reflecting NCEI’s practice of reporting county‑based segments rather than whole‑track totals. [1][2][6][7][9][10]

## Background and synoptic setting

The Sawyerville–Eoline tornado formed during a large and deadly tornado outbreak that affected the southeastern United States from April 25 to 28, 2011, commonly referred to as the 2011 Super Outbreak. A NOAA service assessment reported that the broader outbreak produced 368 confirmed tornadoes, including 224 on April 27 alone, with four EF5, eleven EF4, and twenty‑one EF3 tornadoes, causing approximately 324 fatalities, more than 3,100 injuries, and about 12 billion USD in damage. [4][6]

Within this multi‑day outbreak, April 27, 2011, featured several waves of severe thunderstorms and tornadoes that moved across Alabama from before sunrise into the late evening. NOAA’s GeoPlatform hub describes multiple rounds of tornado‑ and wind‑damage‑producing storms crossing the state over nearly 18 hours, while the NCEI episode narrative notes that this sequence broke the state record for the number of tornadoes in a single day and characterizes it as the most significant tornado outbreak in Alabama’s history. [3][7][9][2]

NWS Birmingham’s historic outbreak summary divides the day into multiple waves of storms and indicates that the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado occurred during the most intense afternoon and evening phase of tornado activity in central Alabama. In statewide summaries of the April 25–28 period, NOAA and NWS note that the combined tornado pathlengths reached about 3,200 miles, the greatest of any documented U.S. tornado outbreak and roughly 600 miles longer than the total pathlength in the 1974 Super Outbreak, underscoring the exceptional scale of the environment that produced the Sawyerville–Eoline event. [2][5][6]

## Meteorological history

According to the NWS Birmingham event review, the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado touched down in far southwest Greene County, Alabama, near the Tombigbee River just west of the community of Tishabee along County Road 69 near Head Drive during the late afternoon or early evening of April 27, 2011. The NWS outbreak summary provides start coordinates of 32.6152°N, 88.0543°W and an approximate start time of 5:30 p.m. Central Daylight Time. From its point of origin, the tornado moved generally east‑northeast across Greene, Hale, and Bibb counties before dissipating east of West Blocton in northeastern Bibb County. [1][2]

Survey information from NWS Birmingham describes the tornado as a long‑tracked, strong tornado rated EF3 on the Enhanced Fujita scale, with an estimated maximum wind speed of 145 mph. The same sources give a total damage path length of 72.13 miles and a maximum path width of 1,760 yards (about 1 mile) for the track across Greene, Hale, and Bibb counties, and they list 7 fatalities and 50 injuries associated with the event. Tornado Talk’s overview of the April 25–28 outbreak classifies the Sawyerville–Eoline event as one of the twenty‑one EF3 tornadoes confirmed during the Super Outbreak. [1][2][6][9]

The NWS narrative notes that the tornado’s intensity evolved along its path. In Greene County, damage initially corresponded to EF1 intensity but increased to EF2 as the tornado progressed, damaging at least four mobile homes, three outbuildings, and two churches, and uprooting or snapping numerous trees. As the tornado crossed the Black Warrior River into Hale County, it intensified further, reaching EF3 strength near and just north of Sawyerville and maintaining EF3 intensity in and near Eoline, before weakening to EF2 south of West Blocton and gradually dissipating near Marvel. [1][7]

NCEI’s Storm Events database provides a county‑specific entry (Event ID 314742) for the Hale County segment of the tornado, listing an EF3 rating, a segment path length of 25.18 miles, a maximum width of 1,760 yards, and a start and end time from 4:43 p.m. to 5:20 p.m. local time, with the segment beginning about 4.92 miles west of Sawyerville and ending about 3.64 miles east‑southeast of Phipps. In contrast, the NWS Birmingham outbreak table aggregates the full multi‑county track under a single “Sawyerville–Eoline (Greene, Hale and Bibb Counties)” entry, which explains the longer 72.13‑mile pathlength and higher casualty totals listed in that source. [7][2]

## Damage and impacts

Damage surveys and official databases indicate that the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado caused significant structural damage along much of its path, particularly in and near the communities of Sawyerville, Harper Hill, and Eoline. The NWS Birmingham report states that hundreds of structures were damaged or destroyed, including numerous mobile homes and single‑family residences, one church, and the Eoline Fire Department, and that thousands of trees were downed along the track from Greene County into Bibb County. [1][7][10]

In Greene County, the tornado’s early stages produced EF1 to EF2 damage. NWS Birmingham documents that near Tishabee, the tornado damaged or destroyed at least four mobile homes, three outbuildings, and two churches and caused extensive tree damage, with two minor injuries reported in this county. This initial damage reflects the intensifying phase of the tornado before it crossed the Black Warrior River toward Hale County. [1]

The most severe damage in Hale County occurred as the tornado crossed Alabama Route 14 just north of Sawyerville and continued through rural areas toward Harper Hill and Ingram. Both the NWS survey and the NCEI event narrative state that the tornado intensified to EF3 strength near Route 14, with estimated winds of 145 mph, and that it caused extensive structural damage along County Roads 18 and 21, Alabama Highway 69 south of Harper Hill, and County Road 29 east of Ingram. Numerous mobile homes and single‑family houses, along with one church, were damaged or destroyed in these locations, and thousands of trees were knocked down as the tornado moved toward the Talladega National Forest and the Bibb County line. [1][7]

In the Eoline area of Bibb County, the tornado again produced EF3‑level damage. NWS Birmingham reports that numerous mobile homes and single‑family homes sustained minor to major damage, that about a dozen such dwellings were destroyed, and that both the Eoline Fire Department and another business were destroyed. The same report notes that one fatality occurred in a vehicle near the fire station and that at least ten other injuries were recorded in this portion of the track. ArcGIS StoryMaps material similarly emphasizes that the most intense damage occurred near Sawyerville, Harper Hill, and Eoline, where extensive structural damage included complete destruction of some buildings. [1][10]

As the tornado moved northeast across southern Bibb County, it weakened to EF2 intensity while crossing Alabama Highway 5 south of West Blocton. According to the NWS survey, several mobile homes and single‑family homes near County Road 26 suffered major damage or were destroyed, and thousands of trees were downed along this part of the path, before the tornado finally lifted just northeast of Marvel. This later stage maintained a damaging but less intense character compared with the EF3 segments near Sawyerville and Eoline. [1]

The NCEI Storm Events entry for the Hale County segment of the tornado lists property damage of 17 million USD, with zero crop damage recorded, along with the destruction or damage of numerous residences, one church, and extensive timber losses. Although NCEI does not provide a single dollar figure for the entire multi‑county track, the Hale County estimate and the qualitative descriptions of heavy damage in Greene and Bibb counties indicate that the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado had a substantial economic impact on the affected rural communities. [7][1][10]

## Casualties

Official sources attribute multiple fatalities and dozens of injuries to the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado. The NWS Birmingham event page and its historic outbreak summary list a total of 7 fatalities and 50 injuries for the full track across Greene, Hale, and Bibb counties. An AL.com retrospective timeline similarly lists the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado as an EF3 event with 145 mph winds, affecting Greene, Hale, and Bibb counties with a 72.13‑mile track, and it assigns 7 deaths and 50 injuries to this tornado. [1][2][9]

By contrast, the NCEI Storm Events database entry for Event ID 314742, which covers only the Hale County portion of the track, lists 6 direct deaths and 40 direct injuries. The NWS and NCEI narratives indicate that these fatalities and injuries in Hale County occurred primarily in and around Sawyerville, Harper Hill, and Ingram, where extensive structural damage to mobile homes, single‑family houses, and a church accompanied the tornado’s EF3 phase. The difference between the Hale County segment figures and the full‑track totals reflects NCEI’s practice of reporting county‑segmented events, while NWS Birmingham aggregates casualties for the entire multi‑county Sawyerville–Eoline tornado. [7][1][2]

The NWS survey further notes that in the Eoline area of Bibb County, one fatality occurred in a vehicle near the destroyed Eoline Fire Department and that at least ten injuries were recorded in that segment of the path. Taken together, these reports indicate that fatal and non‑fatal injuries were distributed along the track, with concentrations in heavily damaged residential areas in both Hale and Bibb counties. [1][7]

## Warnings, emergency response, and immediate aftermath

NWS Birmingham reports that tornado warnings were in effect for Greene, Hale, and Bibb counties from 4:50 p.m. until 7:45 p.m. on April 27, 2011, covering the period during which the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado moved across the area. These warnings were issued as part of a broader sequence of watches and warnings associated with the afternoon and evening supercell thunderstorms that produced multiple tornadoes in central Alabama that day. [1][2][3]

The detailed NWS damage survey describes post‑storm inspection of multiple tracks across Greene, Hale, and Bibb counties, noting that meteorologists and survey teams documented consistent evidence of a strong, long‑tracked tornado along the route from near Tishabee through Sawyerville and Eoline to near Marvel. The survey notes the destruction of the Eoline Fire Department and another business, as well as damage to churches and numerous homes, indicating that local emergency services and community facilities were directly affected by the tornado. However, available sources focus primarily on meteorological and damage assessment aspects, and they provide limited detail on the organization of search‑and‑rescue operations or longer‑term relief efforts specific to this tornado. [1][4][7]

## Place in the 2011 Super Outbreak

In the context of the April 25–28, 2011 Super Outbreak, the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado is classified in NOAA‑linked summaries as an Alabama EF3 tornado that occurred on April 27 among the outbreak’s strong and violent events. Tornado Talk’s overview lists a specific write‑up titled “Sawyerville–Eoline, AL EF3 Tornado – April 27, 2011,” indicating that this tornado is treated as a distinct long‑track event within broader analyses of the outbreak. [2][3][6]

NOAA and NWS summaries describe the April 27 outbreak in Alabama as part of a historic tornado day that produced 316 fatalities across the Southeast, including 234 in Alabama, more than 2,400 injuries, and over 4.2 billion USD in damage. The same summaries state that during the 24‑hour period ending at 12:00 UTC on April 28, the number of tornadoes exceeded the previous 24‑hour record set during the April 3–4, 1974 Super Outbreak, and they characterize the April 27–28 event as the deadliest U.S. tornado outbreak since the March 18, 1925 Tri‑State tornado. Within this framework, the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado is one of several deadly tornadoes that struck Alabama on April 27, alongside higher‑rated events such as the Hackleburg EF5 and the Tuscaloosa–Birmingham EF4, but it remains notable as a long‑tracked EF3 that caused seven deaths and extensive damage in smaller communities and rural areas. [4][5][2][6][7][9]

## Notes on data sources and discrepancies

Different official sources provide varying quantitative figures for the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado because of differing reporting conventions. NWS Birmingham’s event page and historic outbreak table treat the tornado as a single, continuous track across Greene, Hale, and Bibb counties, listing an EF3 rating, a path length of 72.13 miles, a maximum width of 1,760 yards, and casualty totals of 7 fatalities and 50 injuries for the overall event. In contrast, the NCEI Storm Events database records a Hale County segment (Event ID 314742) with an EF3 rating, a 25.18‑mile segment length, the same 1,760‑yard maximum width, and 6 direct deaths and 40 direct injuries, along with 17 million USD in property damage for that county. The coexistence of these figures reflects NCEI’s county‑segmented cataloguing compared with NWS Birmingham’s whole‑track reporting, and both sets of values are cited in this article where relevant. [1][2][7][9]
