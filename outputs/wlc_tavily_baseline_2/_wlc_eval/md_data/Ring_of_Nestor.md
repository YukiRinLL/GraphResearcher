# Ring of Nestor

## Overview and Significance

The Ring of Nestor is a gold signet ring attributed to the Late Bronze Age Aegean world and catalogued in the Corpus der Minoischen und Mykenischen Siegel (CMS) as CMS VI no. 277. It is widely discussed as a Creto–Mycenaean object whose engraved imagery and manufacturing techniques align with known Minoan and Mycenaean glyptic traditions of the 16th–15th centuries BC. Recent scholarship treats the ring as an authentic Late Bronze Age gold signet rather than a modern forgery, situating it among the largest and most complex pictorial compositions known in Aegean glyptic art. [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8]

The ring’s significance lies principally in three areas. First, its large size and high-quality craftsmanship place it within an elite repertoire of gold signet rings that were closely associated with status and ritual practice in the early Mycenaean period, alongside other rich grave assemblages such as the Griffin Warrior tomb at Pylos, which contained four gold signet rings with Minoan ritual scenes. Second, its complex iconography, organized around a "tree of the world" and a guardian creature now interpreted as a scorpion, is used to explore themes of protection, cosmology, and ritual in Aegean religion. Third, the object has become central to debates about authenticity and forgery in Aegean archaeology, prompting detailed reassessment of earlier 20th‑century suspicions and contributing to broader discussions about Minoan–Mycenaean interaction and the transmission of religious imagery. [1][2][3][4][5][6][7][8]

## Identification and Nomenclature

The object conventionally known as the "Ring of Nestor" is identified in the Corpus der Minoischen und Mykenischen Siegel as CMS VI no. 277, and is consistently described in modern scholarship under this sobriquet. Both A. G. Vlachopoulos and other recent authors refer to it as the "so‑called Ring of Nestor", indicating awareness that the name is traditional rather than a secure historical designation. The CMS number links the ring to the series of Aegean sealings and signet rings documented in that corpus, where it is classified among large Aegean gold signet rings. [1][2][9][3][8][10]

The name "Ring of Nestor" was already in circulation in Arthur J. Evans’s early 20th‑century study "The ring of Nestor", which discussed a sepulchral treasure of gold signet rings and bead seals and linked the object to Minoan culture and sites such as Thisbê in Boeotia and Kakóvatos in western Peloponnese. Evans’s usage helped anchor the ring in scholarly discourse and associated it loosely with the Homeric figure Nestor, though the ring itself predates the Homeric epics and no primary textual source connects the artifact directly to the historical or legendary ruler of Pylos. Later scholarship usually keeps the epithet while stressing its conventional nature and the absence of firm evidence that the ring belonged to an individual named Nestor. [1][2][9][3][8][10]

Modern descriptive metadata and image captions in online repositories also employ the "Ring of Nestor" label, sometimes adding information on cultural period and find context. One such caption describes it as a "Ring of Nestor" gold signet ring of the Late Minoan period, dated approximately to 1700–1450 BC, and refers to it as a "Minoan Kakovatos signet ring". This wording reflects a broader tendency to connect the ring with Minoan glyptic traditions and with Kakovatos in Messenia, a site known for rich Early Mycenaean burials displaying strong Cretan influence. At the same time, scholars emphasize that the sobriquet and the proposed find association remain part of an interpretive tradition, not an established excavation record, and continue to reference the ring via its CMS catalog number when discussing technical and iconographic issues. [1][2][9][3][8][10]

## Archaeological and Historical Context

The Ring of Nestor is generally situated within the broader archaeological and historical landscape of Bronze Age Messenia and the Palace of Nestor at Pylos, although no single excavation report in the available sources ties the ring unambiguously to a specific grave or stratigraphic context. Pylos, located on the hill of Ano Englianos in southwestern Greece, was a major Mycenaean palatial centre between roughly 1600 and 1200 BC, covering an area of about 20–30 hectares. Archaeological work has shown that monumental structures were constructed on the hill by around 1500 BC, and that a final complex known as the Palace of Nestor arose after earlier buildings burned around 1400 BC; this palace was destroyed by fire around 1180 BC, at which point its clay administrative tablets were baked and preserved. Excavations in the 1950s and 1960s revealed an archive complex containing the largest collection of Linear B tablets discovered in a single location, providing detailed evidence for social stratification, economic administration, and the territorial division of Pylos into Hither and Further provinces ruled by a wanax (king) and supported by officials such as the lawagetas. [1][2][4][5][6][7][8]

The Palace of Nestor is described as the best-preserved centre of Mycenaean civilization and is surrounded by a rich mortuary landscape of chamber tombs, a grave circle, and multiple tholos (beehive) tombs. All excavated burials in the immediate vicinity of the palace are characterized as elite, with material culture attesting to considerable wealth. Tholos IV, near the palace, is regarded as one of the oldest tholos tombs on the mainland and dates to the Shaft Grave period, contemporary with grave circles at Mycenae. The grave circle at Pylos shows funerary practices with clear Minoan antecedents, such as the use of large pithoi as burial jars, adapted locally by interring multiple individuals in a single jar—a practice not observed on Crete. Across these contexts, grave assemblages contain gold, silver, bronze, ivory, and numerous precious stones, used for jewellery, vessels, weapons, and seal stones, highlighting the social prominence of signet rings and seals in Mycenaean elite display and ritual. [1][2][4][5][6][7][8]

Within this landscape, the discovery in 2015 of the Griffin Warrior’s tomb near Tholos Tomb IV has renewed interest in early Mycenaean funerary practice and its relationships with Minoan culture. The University of Cincinnati team uncovered a large stone-built shaft grave of Late Helladic IIA date close to the Palace of Nestor, containing the undisturbed burial of a single adult male, aged about 30–35 years, accompanied by hundreds of artifacts of gold, silver, bronze, ivory, and semiprecious stones. Many of these objects were manufactured in the Minoan world, and the grave included four gold signet rings bearing Minoan ritual imagery such as bull‑leaping scenes. The assemblage has been interpreted as evidence for complex belief systems that intertwined Minoan ritual traditions with emerging Mycenaean customs and for the role of imported or hybrid iconography in the construction of elite identities and religious practice. [1][2][4][5][6][7][8]

Although the Ring of Nestor itself is not explicitly listed among the grave goods of the Griffin Warrior or of a specific excavated tomb at Pylos in the available sources, scholars discussing the ring place it within this broader milieu of Creto–Mycenaean interaction, elite burial, and palatial society. Its classification as a large Aegean gold signet ring and the characterization of its iconography as comparable to other Late Bronze Age glyptic art suggest that it belonged to networks of ritual objects and personal insignia that circulated among Mycenaean elites in regions such as Messenia, perhaps including sites like Kakovatos and Pylos where strong Cretan influence is documented in architecture and grave goods. [1][2][4][5][6][7][8]

## Physical Description and Iconography

The Ring of Nestor is described in modern iconographic studies as a large gold signet ring of the Aegean Late Bronze Age, measuring approximately 3.29 cm by 2.18 cm and ranking as the fifth largest Aegean signet ring in at least one comparative dataset. Its material is gold, and stylistic and manufacturing details have been argued to align with other Creto–Mycenaean artifacts, supporting its attribution to the 16th–15th centuries BC. Technical analyses emphasize the ring’s fine craftsmanship and the complexity of its engraved surface, placing it within a group of unique signet rings from the first centuries of the Late Bronze Age whose workmanship has been the subject of detailed study. [1][2][4][11][12][13]

The engraved scene on the ring’s bezel is characterized by scholars as one of the most populous and intricate compositions in Aegean glyptic art, organized around an old "tree of the world" whose branches host numerous figures and motifs. Arthur Evans originally described a central zoomorphic figure as a "watch‑dog" or "dragon" guarding this world tree. In more recent interpretations, this guardian motif is re‑identified as a scorpion, which is proposed to be a major apotropaic (protective) symbol in Bronze Age Aegean iconography. Within this reading, the scorpion is said to "kill" the so‑called "Minoan dragon" on the ring, providing a narrative framework in which the scorpion’s protective function structures the overall scene. The composition as a whole is understood as a ritual or religious tableau, though the available summaries do not list all individual figures, animals, or implements in detail, and some elements remain the subject of interpretive debate. [1][2][4][11][12][13]

The scene has also been related to broader ritual themes by analogy with other contemporary signet rings and glyptic imagery. A news report on a gold signet ring discovered near the Palace of Nestor at Pylos—described as Mycenaean and bearing a detailed scene of ritual sacrifice with extremely fine, possibly microscopic engraving—illustrates the level of craftsmanship and ritual focus typical of high‑status gold rings in this region. Similarly, discussions of other large Aegean signet rings, such as CMS I 179 from the Tiryns treasure, underline the capacity of such rings to carry complex symbolic programs involving deities, fertility motifs, and cosmological elements like sun and moon. While these other rings differ in specific content, their combination of large size, gold material, and dense imagery provides a comparative framework for understanding the Ring of Nestor as part of an elite repertoire of ritual signet rings. [1][2][4][11][12][13]

In addition to the central guardian and world tree, iconographic analyses of the Ring of Nestor emphasize the scene’s place within early Late Bronze Age visual traditions. Vlachopoulos notes that recent finds of wall‑paintings, gold rings, figurines, and ivories with comparable iconographic subjects have reinforced the view that the ring’s imagery belongs to an authentic Late Bronze Age repertoire rather than a modern invention. Eliopoulos’s study of the ring’s iconography similarly revisits key features and comparisons, aiming to situate its motifs within Minoan and Mycenaean art and to contribute to ongoing debates over the ring’s significance. Together, these studies portray the Ring of Nestor as a large gold signet ring with a densely populated ritual scene centred on a world tree and a protective scorpion motif, executed with high technical skill and integrated into broader Aegean iconographic traditions of the 16th–15th centuries BC. [1][2][4][11][12][13]

## Discovery History and Provenance

The precise discovery history and provenance of the Ring of Nestor, including a securely documented findspot, excavation date, and museum accession number, are not fully established in the sources available here. Modern scholarship acknowledges that the circumstances in which the ring and certain comparable Creto–Mycenaean gold rings were found are "obscure – even legendary", and notes that this ambiguity historically encouraged some scholars to treat them as forgeries. A. G. Vlachopoulos explicitly describes his study as addressing erroneous identifications of such rings as fakes and as documenting the authenticity of the so‑called Ring of Nestor (CMS VI no. 277), implying that the evidence base consists of a combination of documentary references, stylistic and technical analysis, and comparison with securely excavated material rather than a single, comprehensive excavation report. [1][2][9][4][3][8][5][6]

Early 20th‑century discussion of the ring appears in Arthur J. Evans’s publication "The ring of Nestor", which is presented as a study of a sepulchral treasure of gold signet rings and bead seals associated with sites such as Thisbê in Boeotia and Kakóvatos in Messenia. The metadata for this work list keywords including Nestor, Minoan culture, and Kakóvatos, and refer to the object as part of a sepulchral treasure, suggesting that Evans linked the ring to funerary contexts and to a wider assemblage of rings. However, the brief metadata do not specify a precise excavation trench, stratigraphic locus, or museum accession number for the ring. Later scholarship has often cited Evans’s study when referencing the ring, but modern authors emphasize that its early publication history and narrative framing leave room for uncertainty about the exact circumstances of discovery. [1][2][9][4][3][8][5][6]

In more recent descriptive material, an online caption refers to a "Ring of Nestor" signet ring of the Late Minoan period (c. 1700–1450 BC), identified as a "Minoan Kakovatos signet ring". This suggests that at least some modern cataloguing traditions associate the ring with Kakovatos in western Messenia, a site known for rich Early Mycenaean burials with strong Cretan influence. Yet the caption does not provide a detailed excavation reference, and it remains uncertain whether this association reflects a documented findspot or a retrospective linkage based on stylistic and regional considerations. [1][2][9][4][3][8][5][6]

Technical studies of the ring’s authenticity and iconography further underline the limited nature of the provenance evidence. Ingo Pini’s article on the ring’s authenticity, as summarized in an online PDF, notes that debates have focused on both iconography and the possibility of forgery by restorers or craftsmen such as the Gillierons, and that new technical observations are brought forward to support the ring’s ancient origin. Vlachopoulos’s study similarly strengthens the authenticity case through comparison with other Late Bronze Age rings, wall‑paintings, figurines, and ivories, as well as through analysis of the ring’s manufacturing details. These approaches rely on internal features of the object and external comparanda rather than on a detailed excavation record, and they collectively acknowledge that the ring’s discovery context is not documented with the same clarity as, for example, the Griffin Warrior’s tomb at Pylos, whose location, stratigraphy, and grave goods are thoroughly published. [1][2][9][4][3][8][5][6]

Taken together, the available sources indicate that the Ring of Nestor was part of a sepulchral treasure of gold rings and beads known to Evans, that later cataloguing traditions associate it with Kakovatos and Late Minoan or early Mycenaean contexts, and that modern scholars classify it as a Creto–Mycenaean gold signet ring of the 16th–15th centuries BC. At the same time, they explicitly acknowledge that the ring’s provenance rests on fragmented documentary references and stylistic analysis, and that no single published excavation report currently ties the named Ring of Nestor to a precise findspot, discovery date, and museum accession number. [1][2][9][4][3][8][5][6]

## Scholarly Interpretations and Debates

Scholarly interpretations of the Ring of Nestor focus on three interconnected themes: iconographic readings of its complex imagery, debates over its authenticity, and its place within discussions of Aegean religion and Minoan–Mycenaean interaction. [1][2][9][4][11][12][5][8][14][15][10]

Iconographic readings [1][2][9][4][11][12][5][8][14][15][10]

Arthur J. Evans’s early account of the ring described the principal zoomorphic figure on the bezel as a "watch‑dog" or "dragon" guarding an old "tree of the world", whose branches carry one of the most populous scenes in Aegean glyptic art. This description situates the ring within a cosmological framework in which a world tree and its guardian structure a crowded ritual tableau. Later iconographic studies have revisited this scene. A. G. Vlachopoulos identifies the guardian motif as a scorpion, arguing that scorpions functioned as major apotropaic (protective) symbols in Bronze Age Aegean imagery and that, in the ring’s composition, the scorpion "kills" the so‑called Minoan dragon. In his interpretation, this act provides a coherent narrative for the ring’s imagery, emphasizing protection and the neutralization of a threatening creature at the foot of the world tree. [1][2][9][4][11][12][5][8][14][15][10]

The overall scene is generally understood as a ritual or religious composition, though detailed listings of every figure and attribute remain limited in the accessible summaries. Comparative work on other large Aegean signet rings, such as CMS I 179 from the Tiryns treasure, shows that such rings can depict female deities, fertility symbols like grain, birds associated with Zeus, seasonal cycles, and sun‑moon backgrounds that express ideas of eternity. While these motifs do not necessarily appear in identical form on the Ring of Nestor, their presence on contemporary rings illustrates how large gold signet rings could encode complex religious themes. Eliopoulos’s study "Some Observations on the Iconography of the ‘Ring of Nestor’" explicitly reassesses the ring’s motifs within the context of Aegean forgeries and authenticity debates and examines elements such as incurved altars and other features to evaluate how its imagery fits within Minoan and Mycenaean artistic traditions. [1][2][9][4][11][12][5][8][14][15][10]

Authenticity debates [1][2][9][4][11][12][5][8][14][15][10]

From the early 20th century onward, scholars have disagreed about whether the Ring of Nestor is an authentic Late Bronze Age artifact or a modern forgery. Evans’s dramatic framing of the ring as part of a sepulchral treasure and the lack of a clear excavation record contributed to a climate in which some researchers suspected that restorers or craftsmen such as the Gillierons might have produced sophisticated modern imitations. Pini’s article on the ring’s authenticity, as summarized in an online PDF, notes that debates have focused on both iconography and technical aspects and that new observations about stylistic and manufacturing details align the ring with known Minoan–Mycenaean artifacts, supporting its ancient origin. [1][2][9][4][11][12][5][8][14][15][10]

Vlachopoulos’s 2020 study "The Ring of Nestor and the quest for authenticity" offers the most systematic recent reassessment. It explicitly aims to document the authenticity of the so‑called Ring of Nestor (CMS VI no. 277) and to discuss cases in which Creto–Mycenaean gold rings have been erroneously identified as fakes. Vlachopoulos notes that earlier scholarly tendencies to treat rings such as the Ring of Nestor and the Ring of Minos as forgeries were underpinned by the obscure or legendary circumstances of their reported discovery. He argues that this tendency has now been completely reassessed in favour of authenticity, citing recent finds of wall‑paintings, gold rings, figurines, and ivories with comparable iconographic subjects, as well as detailed studies of the techniques and craftsmanship of signet rings from the first centuries of the Late Bronze Age (16th–15th centuries BC). These comparanda, combined with technical analyses, underpin a growing consensus in the literature used here that the Ring of Nestor is best understood as an authentic Creto–Mycenaean gold signet ring. [1][2][9][4][11][12][5][8][14][15][10]

Religion and Minoan–Mycenaean interaction [1][2][9][4][11][12][5][8][14][15][10]

The ring’s imagery and debated history have made it an important case study in discussions of Aegean religion and Minoan–Mycenaean cultural interaction. The Griffin Warrior tomb at Pylos, discovered in 2015 and dated to Late Helladic IIA, contained four gold signet rings with Minoan ritual scenes among hundreds of luxury grave goods manufactured in the Minoan world. Analyses of that assemblage interpret the rings’ iconography as significant for understanding how Mycenaean elites adopted and adapted Minoan ritual practices, and suggest that complex belief systems intertwined Minoan traditions with Mycenaean customs. Within this framework, the Ring of Nestor is approached as another high‑status gold signet ring whose densely populated scene may reflect similar processes of religious borrowing, hybridization, and elite self‑representation. [1][2][9][4][11][12][5][8][14][15][10]

Broader discussions of Bronze Age social and religious identities emphasize the role of personal ornaments and ritual objects—including signet rings, seal stones, jewellery, and fresco imagery—in constructing embodied identities and articulating status. Studies of Mycenaean palatial centres show that palaces like that at Pylos served as nodes of economic redistribution, political authority, and ritual performance, embedded in mortuary landscapes of elite tombs whose material culture demonstrates great wealth and strong external connections. Within such settings, gold signet rings with complex ritual imagery are interpreted as markers of elite status, potential instruments of administrative or ceremonial practice, and vehicles for expressing cosmological and protective themes. [1][2][9][4][11][12][5][8][14][15][10]

Association with Nestor and social function [1][2][9][4][11][12][5][8][14][15][10]

Despite the ring’s traditional association with the name Nestor, modern scholarship available here offers limited explicit discussion of any direct connection between the artifact and the Homeric ruler of Pylos or of its specific social function beyond general categories such as elite ornament and ritual object. Evans’s title and later descriptive labels, such as "Ring of Nestor" and "Cup of Nestor", reflect a broader habit of linking prominent Bronze Age objects from sites like Mycenae and Pylos to Homeric figures by analogy, but they do not rest on documentary proof of ownership. Current studies therefore treat the sobriquet as conventional and focus instead on what the ring’s material, size, and imagery suggest about its place in Aegean religious iconography and in networks of Minoan–Mycenaean interaction, rather than on reconstructing a specific historical individual’s relationship to the ring. [1][2][9][4][11][12][5][8][14][15][10]

## Cultural Reception and Influence

Direct evidence for the broader cultural reception or influence of the Ring of Nestor beyond specialist archaeological and art‑historical circles is limited in the material available here. The ring’s name nonetheless participates in a wider tradition of associating striking Bronze Age objects with Homeric figures, as seen for example in the gold stemmed cup from Mycenae that is commonly called the "Cup of Nestor" following Homer’s description and which is displayed in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens. This naming practice reflects the projection of epic identities onto archaeological finds and has contributed to the ring’s recognizability in scholarly and popular discourse, even though no direct historical connection with Nestor can be documented. [1][2][11][4][15][3][10]

Within specialist literature, the Ring of Nestor functions as a reference point in debates over Aegean forgeries, authenticity, and iconography. Eliopoulos’s article "Some Observations on the Iconography of the ‘Ring of Nestor’" and Vlachopoulos’s "The Ring of Nestor and the quest for authenticity" both engage with the ring as a key case study for examining how scholars assess the genuineness and meaning of Creto–Mycenaean gold rings. Pini’s technical study further integrates the ring into discussions of stylistic and manufacturing criteria for authenticity. These works show that the ring has influenced methodological debates about the evaluation of glyptic artifacts and the role of iconographic analysis in establishing authenticity and cultural attribution. [1][2][11][4][15][3][10]

Outside formal publications, the ring appears in online image collections and social media‑style platforms. A captioned image identifies a "Ring of Nestor" gold signet ring of the Late Minoan period (c. 1700–1450 BC), described as a "Minoan Kakovatos signet ring" and grouped with tags such as "gold intaglio" and "Minoan ring". Such presentations indicate that the ring is used as a visual exemplar of Minoan or Aegean gold rings in broader online contexts, contributing to public awareness of Aegean Bronze Age art even when detailed scholarly information is not provided. However, these formats do not typically explore the ring’s historical significance or social function in depth. [1][2][11][4][15][3][10]

In sum, the available sources show that the Ring of Nestor has a modest but distinct cultural presence: it is invoked by name in specialist debates on authenticity and iconography, participates in Homeric naming traditions for Bronze Age objects, and circulates in online visual culture as an image of an elaborate Minoan or Creto–Mycenaean gold ring. Evidence remains limited for any extensive influence on broader literature, art, or media beyond these domains. [1][2][11][4][15][3][10]

## Limitations of the Evidence

The evidence base for the Ring of Nestor, as represented in the sources available here, presents several significant limitations that affect how securely the object can be contextualized and interpreted. [1][2][9][4][11][12][8][15][10]

First, the ring’s discovery history and provenance are only partially documented. Modern scholarship explicitly notes that the circumstances in which the Ring of Nestor and some comparable Creto–Mycenaean gold rings were found are "obscure – even legendary", and that this ambiguity historically encouraged some scholars to treat them as forgeries. Evans’s early study "The ring of Nestor" frames the ring as part of a sepulchral treasure of gold signet rings and bead seals linked to places such as Thisbê and Kakóvatos, but the brief metadata consulted here do not provide specific excavation details, stratigraphic information, or a museum accession number. Later descriptive captions associate the ring with Kakovatos and with the Late Minoan period, yet they do not cite a definitive excavation report. On this basis, it is not possible, using the present sources, to trace the Ring of Nestor from discovery through excavation to museum curation with the level of precision achieved for graves such as the Griffin Warrior’s tomb at Pylos. [1][2][9][4][11][12][8][15][10]

Second, while technical and stylistic analyses strongly support the ring’s authenticity as a Late Bronze Age gold signet ring, they necessarily rely on internal characteristics and external comparanda rather than on secure provenance. Pini’s article and Vlachopoulos’s study bring forward new technical observations and comparisons with known Minoan–Mycenaean artifacts, arguing that the ring’s manufacturing details and iconography align with authentic 16th–15th century BC signet rings. These arguments have led to a broad reassessment in favour of authenticity, but they cannot wholly remove uncertainties arising from the lack of a fully documented find context. [1][2][9][4][11][12][8][15][10]

Third, the available summaries provide only partial information about the ring’s iconography. They emphasize the central world tree and guardian motif, the reidentification of the latter as a scorpion, and the interpretation of the scorpion as an apotropaic symbol that "kills" a Minoan dragon. However, they do not enumerate all figures, animals, and attributes in the scene, and some comparative motifs discussed in relation to other rings—such as deities, seasonal symbols, or specific sacrificial elements—are presented as analogies rather than as securely identified components of the Ring of Nestor’s imagery. This limits the precision with which the ring’s ritual narrative and religious meaning can be reconstructed. [1][2][9][4][11][12][8][15][10]

Fourth, there is limited explicit discussion in the available sources of the ring’s association with the Homeric figure Nestor or of its specific social function. Evans’s title and later conventional naming practices link the ring symbolically to Nestor, much as a gold stemmed cup from Mycenae is called the "Cup of Nestor" following Homer’s description. Yet no primary textual or archaeological evidence is cited to show that the ring was owned or used by a historical or legendary Nestor, and modern scholarship generally treats the epithet as traditional rather than evidentiary. Similarly, while the ring is recognized as a large gold signet ring belonging to an elite repertoire of ritual or status objects, detailed analysis of its day‑to‑day social function—whether as a seal, a ritual implement, a heirloom, or a purely symbolic ornament—is not extensively developed in the material accessible here. [1][2][9][4][11][12][8][15][10]

Finally, the sources consulted form only a subset of the wider literature on Aegean glyptic and Bronze Age religion. Key studies by Eliopoulos, Pini, and Vlachopoulos are represented largely through abstracts, metadata, and secondary descriptions rather than through full texts. As a result, nuanced arguments, detailed iconographic catalogues, and technical data that may exist in the complete publications are only partially reflected. Readers should therefore treat the synthesized account as a summary based on limited but reputable sources, and recognize that further specialist literature may refine or revise aspects of the Ring of Nestor’s dating, iconography, and interpretation beyond what can be documented here. [1][2][9][4][11][12][8][15][10]
