- "The 'Ring of Nestor': A Glimpse into the Minoan After-World." Journal of Hellenic Studies 45: 43-75. [4]

- Modern scholarship includes dedicated academic studies that critically examine the Ring of Nestor’s iconography and authenticity within the broader context of Aegean forgeries and Creto‑Mycenaean gold rings. [2]

- The Ring of Nestor is catalogued as CMS VI, no. 277 and is described as the fifth largest known Aegean signet ring, measuring approximately 3.29 cm by 2.18 cm. [1]

- Vlachopoulos argues, on the basis of iconographic comparisons and technical studies of Creto‑Mycenaean gold rings, that the Ring of Nestor (CMS VI, no. 277) is an authentic Late Bronze Age artifact, thereby overturning earlier 1920s claims that it was a modern forgery. [1]

- The authenticity of the so‑called Ring of Nestor has been the subject of long‑running scholarly debate, with past arguments against its ancient origin focusing primarily on iconographic features rather than technical analysis. [2]

- At least one modern study on the Ring of Nestor contends that contradictions and limitations in technical analyses of Aegean artifacts highlight the importance of iconographic study when evaluating authenticity, including for the Ring of Nestor. [2]

- One iconographic study argues that the depiction of incurved altars on the Ring of Nestor and their parallels elsewhere in Aegean art support the ring’s authenticity. [2]

- Vlachopoulos reinterprets the principal pictorial motif on the Ring of Nestor—previously described by Evans as a watch‑dog or dragon guarding a world‑tree—as a scorpion functioning as a major apotropaic symbol in Bronze Age Aegean iconography and symbolically killing the ‘Minoan Dragon’ of the ring. [1]

- A recent study describes how new finds of related iconographic subjects (such as wall paintings, gold rings, figurines, and ivories) and closer study of ring‑making techniques and craftsmanship have strengthened the case for the authenticity of the Ring of Nestor and similar Creto‑Mycenaean signet rings from the early Late Bronze Age. [1]

- One paper warns against the a priori exclusion of unprovenanced or poorly documented ancient and medieval finger rings, including the Ring of Nestor, as potential sources of data for understanding past societies and material culture. [2]

- The study titled "The Pseudo‑Minoan Nestor Ring and Its Egyptian Iconography" treats a ring associated with Nestor that is characterized as pseudo‑Minoan and analyzes its imagery in relation to Egyptian motifs, engaging with earlier interpretations such as Evans’s article "The 'Ring of Nestor': A Glimpse into the Minoan After‑World" and Frazer’s "The Golden Bough". [4]
