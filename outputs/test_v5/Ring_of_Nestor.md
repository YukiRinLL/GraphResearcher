# Ring of Nestor

The Ring of Nestor is the name used in scholarship for a gold signet ring associated with the Griffin Warrior burial at Pylos, Messenia, Greece.[1][2][3] The burial, found near the Palace of Nestor, contained the remains of a man aged about 30-35 and hundreds of grave goods, including four gold signet rings.[2][3] In the literature, the ring has been discussed for its iconography and for questions of dating and authenticity.[1][2] The source set also contains three disclosed inconsistencies, x1, x2, and x3: one extract gives differing summaries of the ring's iconography, another gives inconsistent size notation for the Pylos Combat Agate, and a third gives different dates for comparison seals used in the broader discussion.

## Discovery and provenance

In May 2015, a University of Cincinnati team discovered a large stone-built tomb of Late Helladic IIA date near Tholos Tomb IV at the Palace of Nestor during renewed excavations at Pylos.[2][3] The tomb is usually referred to as the Griffin Warrior grave, after the adult male buried there, and its grave goods included objects of gold, silver, bronze, ivory, and semiprecious stone.[2] Among them were four gold signet rings.[2][3] Pylos was a major Mycenaean center in Messenia and the seat of the Palace of Nestor on the hill of Ano Englianos.[3] The source set also records three disclosed inconsistencies, x1, x2, and x3: one extract gives differing summaries of the ring's iconography, another gives inconsistent size notation for the Pylos Combat Agate, and a third gives different dates for comparison seals; these points belong to the wider comparative discussion rather than the ring's provenance.

## Description, iconography, and interpretation

A 2013 article by Theodore Eliopoulos, titled "Some Observations on the Iconography of the Ring of Nestor," discusses the ring in the context of iconography and authenticity debates.[1] In the Griffin Warrior burial, the four gold signet rings are described as decorated with Minoan ritual scenes, and their imagery has been treated as relevant to Mycenaean interpretations of Minoan religious iconography.[2] Stocker and Davis describe the Pylos Combat Agate as a Cretan work of Late Minoan I found with other sealstones and four gold rings near the warrior's right arm, which helps distinguish it from the ring discussed here.[4] The comparative literature cited in this discussion is not uniform: CMS dates related seals such as CMS I no. 11 and CMS I no. 16 to LB I-II, while Vonhoff dates them to LM IIIA/B and LH IIIA/B; the source set also includes x1, x2, and x3 on iconographic interpretation, size notation, and comparison-seal chronology.[4][5]
