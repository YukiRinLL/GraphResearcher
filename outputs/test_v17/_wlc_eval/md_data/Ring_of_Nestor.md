# Ring of Nestor

The Ring of Nestor is the name commonly given to a gold signet ring that entered scholarly literature in 1925 and became associated with interpretations of Minoan imagery and the afterlife.[1][3] Sir Arthur Evans published a 1925 study titled *The Ring of Nestor: A Glimpse into the Minoan After-World and a Sepulchral Treasure of Gold Signet-Rings and Bead-Seals from Thisbe, Boeotia*.[1][3] The ring’s reported discovery in 1924 and its association with a tholos tomb at Kakovatos near Pylos are not supported in the supplied material by a professionally documented excavation record.[4][15] Its authenticity has received conflicting assessments: Evans accepted it and used its imagery in a Minoan afterlife interpretation, while members of the archaeological board in Athens reportedly rejected it as a modern forgery.[15] A later peer-reviewed article by Nanno Marinatos and Briana Jackson argued that the ring’s butterfly motif weighs against authenticity, although the available abstract does not provide the full iconographic analysis.[4] The reported Kakovatos find context is therefore not equivalent to verified provenance, and the opposing historical assessments do not establish a settled authentication result.[15] These questions place the object within the category *gemma dubitanda*, a technical term for sealstones and signet rings attributed to the Aegean Bronze Age whose provenance is not securely documented.[15]

## Description and reported provenance

The ring is a gold signet ring with an engraved bezel. The supplied sources identify an illustration of it as “Ring of Nestor. CMS VI, 277,” while another record documents Evans’s drawing and a watercolour associated with the object.[22] The ring’s commonly used name derives from its reported association with the Pylos region, identified in the sources with the legendary home of Nestor.[21]

A 2019 study describes the ring as allegedly discovered in a tholos tomb at Kakovatos near Pylos and records its first appearance in scholarly literature in 1925.[15] The same study states that Evans privately purchased the object and published it in the *Journal of Hellenic Studies*, whereas members of the archaeological board in Athens had previously refused to buy it because they considered it a forgery made by a modern artist.[15] The discovery account therefore belongs to the object’s reported history rather than to a securely verified excavation provenance. In the study’s discussion of *gemmae dubitandae*, the absence of professional, well-documented archaeological excavations is given as a reason for withholding confidence in Bronze Age attribution.[15]

## Iconography and interpretation

The ring’s bezel carries a miniature engraved scene. Evans interpreted the pictorial composition as a Minoan Elysian domain in which a human couple journeys to the Beyond and enters the Elysian Fields.[15] In that reading, butterflies and chrysalises could symbolize the human soul; a later account of Evans’s interpretation connects the soul with weighing and possible reincarnation.[15][16] These are historical interpretations attributed to Evans, not an independently established reconstruction of Minoan religious belief.

The object is catalogued as CMS VI, no. 277.[6][9] A 2020 study identifies a pictorial unit previously called Evans’s “watch-dog” or “dragon” as a scorpion and interprets it as an apotropaic figure that kills the ring’s “Minoan Dragon.”[6] Because the accessible material supplies the study’s abstract but not its complete visual analysis, this identification should be treated as that study’s interpretation rather than as an uncontested description. The available record documents Evans’s drawing, a colour watercolour, and comparisons with other imagery, but the supplied excerpts do not provide the full arguments attached to those illustrations.[6]

## Authenticity debate

The authenticity question was present from the object’s first documented scholarly circulation. A source states that the ring was found in 1924 and that Arthur Evans accepted it as authentic, whereas many other scholars condemned it as a forgery.[4] A more specific account reports that members of the archaeological board in Athens refused to buy it because they considered it “a forgery manufactured by a modern artist”; Evans nevertheless privately purchased it and published it in the *Journal of Hellenic Studies*.[15] These were conflicting historical assessments: the board’s judgment and Evans’s publication document opposing positions, but neither fact by itself establishes the object’s modern or Bronze Age manufacture.[15]

Arguments against authenticity include the suspicious circumstances of the reported discovery and the lack of a professionally documented excavation provenance.[4][15] Marinatos and Jackson further argue that the ring’s interpretive coherence depends on Egyptian prototypes and that its butterfly motif weighs against authenticity; the supplied text provides their abstract-level conclusion but not the detailed comparisons.[4] The term *gemma dubitanda* captures this evidentiary position: it refers to sealstones and signet rings attributed to the Aegean Bronze Age whose provenance and authenticity remain uncertain.[15]

The dispute has not been uniform across later writing. The supplied abstract of a 2020 study states that earlier identifications of certain gold rings, including the Ring of Nestor, were reassessed in favour of authenticity on the basis of comparable iconographic finds and more detailed study of technique and craftsmanship.[4] The available material therefore records a later pro-authenticity reassessment alongside continuing uncertainty about the ring’s provenance and manufacture; it does not establish a settled authentication result.[4][15]
