# 2011 Sawyerville–Eoline tornado

## Overview

The 2011 Sawyerville–Eoline tornado was a long-track, violent tornado that struck portions of Greene, Hale, and Bibb Counties in west-central Alabama on 27 April 2011, during the historic late-April 2011 tornado outbreak in the southeastern United States. According to official National Weather Service (NWS) documentation, the tornado was rated EF3 on the Enhanced Fujita scale, with a surveyed damage path length of 72.13 mi (116.1 km) and a maximum path width of 1,760 yd (1.0 mi). The event caused severe damage to homes, businesses, community facilities, and forested areas along its route and resulted in seven fatalities and fifty injuries. [1][2][3]

The tornado is named for the communities of Sawyerville in Hale County and Eoline in Bibb County, which experienced some of the most intense damage, including destruction of residential structures and the local fire department in Eoline. The NWS Birmingham office lists the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado among the significant tornadoes of 27 April 2011, a day on which numerous strong and violent tornadoes affected Alabama. The Alabama Tornado Database for 2011 includes a distinct entry for this tornado, documenting its EF3 rating, multi-county track through Greene, Hale, and Bibb Counties, and associated casualties. [1][2][3]

## Meteorological synopsis

The Sawyerville–Eoline tornado formed within a broader episode of severe convective storms on 27 April 2011, when a powerful storm system produced numerous tornadoes across Alabama and surrounding states. The NWS Birmingham office’s event overview for the "Historic Outbreak of April 27, 2011" documents a series of tornadoes in central Alabama on that date, including long-track, strong, and violent tornadoes associated with supercell thunderstorms. Within this sequence, the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado is catalogued as an EF3 tornado affecting Greene, Hale, and Bibb Counties, with an extended track and substantial casualty figures. [1][2][3]

According to the NWS damage survey specific to the event, the tornado touched down in far southwestern Greene County near the Tombigbee River just west of Tishabee, then moved northeast across rural portions of the county north of Forkland. The survey reports that the tornado subsequently crossed the Black Warrior River into Hale County, west of Sawyerville, and intensified as it approached and crossed Alabama Route 14 north of Sawyerville, reaching EF3 intensity with estimated winds of about 145 mph (233 km/h). As the tornado progressed through Hale County, it crossed local roads including County Roads 18, 21, and 29 and Alabama Highway 69, producing extensive structural damage and numerous casualties. [1][2][3]

Continuing northeastward, the tornado entered the Talladega National Forest near the community of Ingram and tracked toward the Bibb County line, where it caused widespread tree damage consistent with its strong intensity. In Bibb County, the tornado moved through the Eoline area, where the NWS survey describes significant structural damage characteristic of EF3 intensity, with winds again estimated near 145 mph. In Eoline, numerous mobile homes and single-family residences sustained minor to major damage, a dozen such structures were destroyed, and community facilities including the Eoline Fire Department and a nearby business were demolished. The survey notes that the tornado weakened slightly to EF2 intensity with estimated winds near 125 mph (201 km/h) as it crossed Alabama Highway 5 south of West Blocton; several additional mobile homes and homes were heavily damaged or destroyed near County Road 26, and thousands of trees were downed along the later portion of the path before the tornado dissipated just northeast of Marvel. [1][2][3]

The Alabama Tornado Database for 2011 and the NWS event overview both list the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado with a total path length of approximately 72.13 mi and a maximum width of about 1,760 yd, underscoring its status as one of the longer-track tornadoes of that day in central Alabama. The tabular database provides the tornado’s date, time, counties affected, EF rating, path statistics, and casualty totals, while the narrative survey adds detailed geographic and intensity information along the track. [1][2][3]

## Impact and damage

The Sawyerville–Eoline tornado produced severe and varied impacts along its multi-county path, including extensive structural and vegetation damage and multiple casualties. The NWS Birmingham damage survey and the Alabama Tornado Database both attribute seven fatalities and fifty injuries to this tornado, distributed among Greene, Hale, and Bibb Counties. The NWS overview of the "Historic Outbreak of April 27, 2011" also lists these casualty figures for the event in its tabular summary of the day’s tornadoes. [1][2][3]

In Greene County, the tornado initially struck rural areas near the Tombigbee River just west of Tishabee, moving northeast across sparsely populated terrain north of Forkland. The survey notes that at least four mobile homes, three outbuildings, and two churches were damaged or destroyed in this segment of the track, and two minor injuries were reported. Although the early portion of the path affected fewer structures than later segments, the damage to small community facilities and residences in Greene County illustrates the breadth of impacts even at lower population density. [1][2][3]

Upon crossing the Black Warrior River into Hale County, west of Sawyerville, the tornado encountered more developed areas and intensified as it crossed Alabama Route 14 north of Sawyerville, reaching EF3 strength with estimated winds near 145 mph. In this region, the tornado passed over and near local roads including County Roads 18, 21, and 29 and Alabama Highway 69 south of Harper Hill, generating extensive structural damage. The NWS survey reports that at least forty injuries and six fatalities occurred in Hale County as homes and other buildings were destroyed or heavily damaged along this part of the track. Thousands of trees were also uprooted or snapped, reflecting the strength and breadth of the circulation. [1][2][3]

The tornado’s impacts continued into Bibb County, where it moved through the Talladega National Forest near Ingram and then into the Eoline area. In forested sections, the survey describes significant tree damage, while in Eoline the tornado again produced EF3-level destruction, with winds estimated near 145 mph. Numerous mobile homes and single-family houses in Eoline sustained damage ranging from minor to major, and about a dozen such structures were destroyed outright. Community infrastructure in Eoline was also affected: the Eoline Fire Department building and another nearby business were destroyed when the tornado passed through the area. The survey documents one fatality in a vehicle near the fire department and at least ten other injuries in Bibb County, underscoring the localized severity of the event. [1][2][3]

Farther along its path, as the tornado crossed Alabama Highway 5 south of West Blocton, it weakened to EF2 intensity with estimated winds near 125 mph but continued to inflict major damage on several mobile homes and single-family residences near County Road 26. Thousands of additional trees were downed along the later portion of the track before the tornado lifted just northeast of Marvel, at the northeastern end of its surveyed path. Across all three counties, the combination of residential, commercial, community-facility, and forest damage, together with the documented casualty figures, mark the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado as one of the more destructive EF3 events of the April 27 outbreak in central Alabama. The available NWS and database sources do not provide a specific monetary estimate of economic losses for this tornado, so the financial impact can only be inferred from the documented extent of structural and vegetation damage. [1][2][3]

## Aftermath and response

Published National Weather Service documentation and the Alabama Tornado Database primarily focus on the meteorological characteristics and direct impacts of the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado, and they do not provide detailed narrative accounts of the emergency response or long-term recovery specific to this event. Academic and public-health analyses of the broader April 27, 2011 Alabama tornado outbreak, which include this tornado among many others, describe coordinated emergency medical response and public-safety actions across the state but generally treat the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado as part of the aggregate of storms rather than as a separate case study. In these sources, local emergency services, hospitals, and public-health agencies are reported to have managed large numbers of injured persons and fatalities from multiple tornadoes on 27 April, including those in Greene, Hale, and Bibb Counties. [1][2][3][4]

Because the available NWS and database materials emphasize meteorological surveys and damage statistics and the statewide mortality and outbreak studies synthesize impacts across many tornadoes, specific information on community-level rebuilding efforts, assistance programs, or memorials dedicated solely to the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado is not documented in these sources. As a result, while it is clear from official records that emergency responders, medical personnel, and public agencies were engaged in managing the consequences of this tornado along with others in the outbreak, detailed accounts of the response and recovery in the individual communities of Sawyerville, Eoline, and nearby rural areas remain limited in the available non-Wikipedia documentation. [1][2][3][4]

## Context within the 2011 Super Outbreak and data conflicts

The Sawyerville–Eoline tornado occurred during the late-April 2011 tornado outbreak, often referred to as the 2011 Super Outbreak, which affected large portions of the central and eastern United States. Outbreak overviews from Grokipedia and Tornado Talk describe a multi-day sequence of severe thunderstorms and tornadoes from 25 to 28 April 2011, with 27 April representing the single most active and deadly day of the event. Both sources identify Alabama as one of the states hardest hit by strong and violent tornadoes on 27 April, with numerous long-track tornadoes causing widespread destruction and hundreds of fatalities. Within Alabama, the NWS Birmingham "Historic Outbreak of April 27, 2011" page similarly documents many tornadoes of EF2 intensity or greater, including the Sawyerville–Eoline EF3 tornado in Greene, Hale, and Bibb Counties. [5][6][3][1][2]

Although Grokipedia and Tornado Talk agree on the exceptional severity of the 2011 Super Outbreak, their summary statistics differ slightly. Grokipedia reports that the outbreak produced about 362 tornadoes in total, whereas the Tornado Talk overview gives a figure of about 368 tornadoes, reflecting differences in data sources or counting methodology. For 27 April specifically, Grokipedia cites approximately 199 tornadoes, while Tornado Talk estimates about 224 tornadoes for that day. The two sources also report slightly different overall fatality totals, with Grokipedia giving about 321 deaths associated with the outbreak and Tornado Talk listing about 324 fatalities. Despite these minor discrepancies, both accounts consistently portray the outbreak as one of the most significant tornado events in U.S. history and emphasize the major role of Alabama tornadoes, including the Sawyerville–Eoline storm, in contributing to the high casualty and damage figures. [5][6][3][1][2]

The NWS Birmingham outbreak summary for 27 April 2011 provides a more localized operational perspective, listing the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado alongside other significant Alabama tornadoes in a tabular format that includes time, affected counties, EF rating, path length, maximum width, and casualty statistics. This official record aligns with the Alabama Tornado Database for 2011 in identifying the Sawyerville–Eoline tornado as an EF3 event with a long track across three counties, seven fatalities, and fifty injuries, situating it among the more destructive but not highest-rated tornadoes (which included several EF4 and EF5 tornadoes) during the outbreak in central Alabama. [5][6][3][1][2]
