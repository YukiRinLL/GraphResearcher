# Ring of Nestor

## Overview

The Ring of Nestor is a large Aegean Bronze Age gold signet ring, now in the Ashmolean Museum, Oxford, whose engraved bezel shows a complex sequence of ritual scenes that have made it a key object in the study of Minoan and Mycenaean religion and art. The ring is part of a funerary treasure of gold signet rings and bead‑seals said to come from a burial at Thisbê in Boeotia, central Greece, and became widely known through Arthur J. Evans’s 1925 study in the Journal of Hellenic Studies. On stylistic and technical grounds it is generally dated to the early Late Bronze Age, in the Late Minoan I period around the 16th–15th centuries BC. Scholars have emphasized its unusual size, detailed gold engraving, and multi‑scene iconography, which includes a sacred tree, altars, human or divine figures, and a central guardian creature variously interpreted as a watch‑dog, dragon, or scorpion. The ring’s name alludes to the Homeric hero Nestor, reflecting early attempts to connect its imagery with Homeric conceptions of the afterworld, while more recent work has focused on its place within Aegean glyptic art and ongoing debates about its authenticity and meaning. [1][2][3][4][5][6]

## Conflicting interpretations of the guardian creature

A notable point of disagreement in the interpretation of the Ring of Nestor concerns the nature of the guardian figure adjacent to the sacred tree. Evans described this creature as a watch‑dog or dragon that guards what he called the "tree of the world", integrating it into a broader reading of the scene as a vision of the Minoan after‑world. In contrast, Vlachopoulos argues that the figure should be identified as a scorpion, drawing on parallels where scorpions appear as major apotropaic symbols in Bronze Age Aegean iconography. This divergence reflects different approaches to the ring’s imagery, with Evans leaning on mythological analogies and Vlachopoulos emphasizing iconographic and comparative criteria. [2][3][5]
