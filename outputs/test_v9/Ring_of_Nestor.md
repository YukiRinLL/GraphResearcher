# Ring of Nestor

The **Ring of Nestor** is a name associated with an engraved gold signet ring showing ornately dressed figures before an altar. A University of Cincinnati article’s image caption interprets the scene as an ancient ritual.[2] The caption does not identify the object by the name “Ring of Nestor.” The National Archaeological Museum announcement and the University of Cincinnati article do not provide a catalogue record establishing its date, provenance, excavation context, dimensions, or present collection.[1][2] The name connects the ring in public presentation with the Mycenaean kingdom of Pylos in Messenia, whose palace at Ano Englianos was excavated in 1939 and identified in exhibition materials with the Homeric Pylos of King Nestor.[1][2] That contextual connection does not establish that the ring belonged to Nestor or came from the palace.[1][2][5]

## Discovery and archaeological context

Carl W. Blegen and Konstantinos Kourouniotis excavated the Mycenaean palace at Ano Englianos in Messenia in 1939; the National Archaeological Museum and University of Cincinnati accounts identify the complex with the Homeric Pylos of King Nestor.[1][2] The palace was built shortly after 1300 BCE and functioned as the administrative centre of a kingdom controlling almost the whole region of modern Messenia.[1] A major fire destroyed it around 1200 BCE, after which the hill of Ano Englianos was abandoned.[1]

The Linear B tablets name Pylos, represented as *pu-ro*, but do not name Nestor; they mention a *wanax*, translated as “lord”.[5] Approximately 800 tablets were found in the palace archive at Epano Englianos.[5] This evidence supports the Pylos context but does not link the engraved gold ring to the palace, King Nestor, or a specific excavation inventory.[1][2][5] The Pylos Combat Agate is a separate 3.6-by-2.2-centimetre sealstone depicting three warriors, and the Griffin Warrior burial is a separate burial containing jewellery and sealstones; neither is identified as the engraved ring.[2][5]

## Description, interpretation and public display

The University of Cincinnati article’s image caption describes an engraved gold ring showing ornately dressed figures standing before an altar and labels the scene an ancient ritual.[2] The caption does not identify the object as “the Ring of Nestor.” It gives no catalogue number, measurements, secure date, excavation record, provenance, or named museum collection.[2][1] Thus, the caption supports a visual description and a captioned interpretation, but not the object’s formal name or archaeological attribution.[1][2]

The University of Cincinnati article describes the Getty Villa exhibition “The Kingdom of Pylos: Warrior-Princes of Mycenaean Greece” and its “Palace of Nestor” section of finds from the palace and adjacent burials.[2] The article illustrates the engraved gold ring, but does not establish whether the pictured ring came from the palace, an adjacent burial, or another context.[2] The National Archaeological Museum announcement lists Pylos-related antiquities and their captions, but none identifies an object as the Ring of Nestor.[1]

The National Archaeological Museum announcement gives 25 May 2026 as the exhibition opening and 26 May–31 October 2026 as the public exhibition period.[1] These dates refer to different stages of the same exhibition: 25 May 2026 was the opening, while 26 May–31 October 2026 was the stated public period.[1] This distinction is not a contradiction. The ring’s formal name, provenance, date, excavation context, dimensions, and present collection remain unidentified in the cited object descriptions.[1][2]
