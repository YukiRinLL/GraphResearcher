- The gold signet ring known as the 'Ring of Nestor' (CMS I, 179), discovered in Tiryns in December 1915 by Apostolos Arvanitopoulos, measures approximately 3.5 × 5.7 cm with an inner ring diameter of about 2.2 cm, weighs around 82.95 grams, and has been described in modern scholarship as the largest known Mycenaean gold signet ring. [2]

- Recent scholarship interprets the engraving on the Ring of Nestor as depicting a female deity—possibly identifiable with Demeter—accompanied by an eagle associated with Zeus and symbols of fertility such as grain, with the composition emphasizing themes of divinity, nature, and the cyclical essence of existence. [2]

- One modern interpretive strand reads four recurring elements in the Ring of Nestor’s engraving as symbols of the four seasons, with background representations of the sun and moon together expressing concepts of eternity and cyclical time in ancient ritual thought. [2]

- A recent study characterizes the Ring of Nestor as a Mycenaean heirloom (Κειμήλιο), arguing that its preservation and use reflect the importance of ancestral respect and cultural identity among Mycenaeans. [2]

- Theodore Eliopoulos’s 2013 article 'Some Observations on the Iconography of the “Ring of Nestor”' re‑examines the ring’s motifs within the context of Aegean forgeries and authenticity debates, using iconographic comparisons—including links to incurved altars and related cult equipment—to both question and partially support earlier conclusions about the ring’s authenticity and meaning in Minoan and Mycenaean art. [1]

- Eliopoulos’s treatment situates the Ring of Nestor within a broader Aegean scholarly discourse in which generations of researchers have debated the authenticity of both the Nestor and Minos rings, highlighting that inconsistent technical analyses have made iconographic study a central tool in assessing such objects. [1]

- A modern interpretive proposal within the scholarship on the Ring of Nestor suggests that aspects of its iconography may reflect cultural practices of the 1920s, implying that the imagery could encode or be influenced by early 20th‑century contexts rather than (or in addition to) Bronze Age ritual scenes. [1]

- Interpreting the iconography of the Ring of Nestor is described in recent summaries of the scholarship as particularly complex, because it requires extensive archaeological and mythological knowledge and is constrained by limited understanding of the LH II and MH historical contexts, necessitating what has been called 'careful imaginative engagement'. [2]
