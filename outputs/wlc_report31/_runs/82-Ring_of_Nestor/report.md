## "Ring of Nestor" gold ring Pylos Mycenaean description iconography

- Evans presents the Ring of Nestor as part of a sepulchral treasure of gold signet-rings and bead-seals from Thisbê, Boeotia, and describes it as a large gold signet ring with an elongated elliptical bezel engraved in intaglio with a complex composition. [1]

- Evans emphasizes the unusually large dimensions and fine gold work of the Ring of Nestor compared to typical Minoan and Mycenaean rings, highlighting the precise cutting of figures in intaglio and the elaborate composition in several registers on the bezel. [1]

- Evans identifies iconographic elements on the Ring of Nestor's bezel including a female figure on a boat, altars or platforms, sacred trees, baetyls (sacred stones), and multiple female figures engaged in ritual actions within a ceremonial landscape. [1]

- Evans interprets the composition on the Ring of Nestor as a glimpse into the Minoan after-world, suggesting that the imagery reflects notions of the soul's voyage and cultic rites, with priestess or goddess figures attending a soul's journey. [1]

- The Hesperia article by Davis and Stocker reports that the Griffin Warrior tomb near Tholos Tomb IV at Pylos, dated to Late Helladic IIA, contained four gold signet rings decorated with Minoan ritual scenes, and that many grave goods in the tomb were manufactured in the Minoan world. [2]

- In their comparative discussion of Minoan ritual imagery on signet rings, Davis and Stocker refer to the Ring of Nestor as a classic example of Minoan-style ceremonial iconography on a mainland-found gold signet ring, and use it as a key comparator when interpreting the Griffin Warrior rings. [2]

- The Cambridge chapter notes that, like the Ring of Nestor, another ring shows a combination of scenes including a female individual on a boat, altars, sacred trees, and baetyls linked to a divine theme. [3]

- The Ring of Nestor is discussed within a chapter entitled 'Fakes and Dubitanda' in a Cambridge University Press volume on Aegean Bronze Age art, placing it among objects whose authenticity or status is being critically examined. [3]

- The Academia.edu paper 'SOME OBSERVATIONS ON THE ICONOGRAPHY OF THE "RING OF NESTOR"' is described as analyzing the ring's iconography in the context of Aegean forgeries and authenticity debates, revisiting Evans's interpretation and comparing the ring's motifs with those on other Minoan and Mycenaean signet rings. [4]

- The Academia.edu-hosted PDF of Davis and Stocker's article provides technical descriptions, including dimensions and materials, for four gold signet rings from the Griffin Warrior tomb and uses the Ring of Nestor as a comparative example within the broader corpus of Minoan-style ritual rings. [5]

- The ResearchGate paper on the 5-priestess signet ring describes an elongated elliptical bezel bearing multiple priestess figures and analyzes the ring's geometry and possible astronomical interpretations of its miniature art composition. [6]

- The Past magazine article notes that among gold artifacts from the Palace of Nestor area, there is a gold signet ring from Tholos IV and that the only other gold signet ring known from this area is the famous Ring of Nestor, purchased by Arthur Evans. [7]

- The Ring of Nestor is a large gold signet ring featuring an elongated elliptical bezel engraved in intaglio, with unusually fine gold work and a complex multi-register composition compared to typical Minoan and Mycenaean rings. [1]

- The bezel of the Ring of Nestor bears a complex ritual or funerary scene including multiple female figures, a female figure on a boat, altars or platforms, sacred trees, and baetyls arranged within a ceremonial landscape. [1][2][3][4]

- Evans interpreted the imagery on the Ring of Nestor as representing the Minoan afterworld and the soul’s voyage accompanied by priestess or goddess figures and cultic rites. [1]

- Evans regarded the Ring of Nestor as manufactured in a Minoan (Cretan) workshop on stylistic and craftsmanship grounds, and modern scholars use its imagery as a classic example of Minoan-style ceremonial iconography on a gold signet ring found on the Greek mainland. [1][2]

- According to Evans, the Ring of Nestor formed part of a sepulchral treasure of gold signet rings and bead seals reportedly found at ancient Thisbê in Boeotia. [1]

- The Ring of Nestor is commonly cited as one of very few gold signet rings associated with the Palace of Nestor at Pylos area and is noted as a famous ring purchased by Arthur Evans, although it was not part of the Griffin Warrior tomb assemblage. [2][7]

- The Ring of Nestor has been placed by modern scholars within debates on Aegean Bronze Age authenticity and forgeries, including discussion in a Cambridge chapter on fakes and dubitanda and in a dedicated paper reassessing its iconography to evaluate its genuineness. [3][4]

- Evans associated the Ring of Nestor conceptually with the Homeric figure Nestor, but this naming and connection lack inscriptional or epigraphic confirmation. [1]

- Modern bibliographical and digital‑library treatments (HathiTrust, Heidelberg, SciSpace, Aegeus) uniformly describe Evans’s study as dealing with the Ring of Nestor within a sepulchral treasure of gold signet rings and bead‑seals from Thisbe in Boeotia, implicitly maintaining the Boeotian tomb context rather than reassigning the ring to a Pylos provenance. [18][27][28][30]

- The Pylos‑focused exhibition and corpus descriptions for Mycenaean gold signet rings discuss rich grave goods from the Pylos region but do not mention the Ring of Nestor, indicating that current Pylos‑region catalogues do not treat this ring as part of the documented Pylian assemblages. [26]

- The UC Classics Nestor database functions as a bibliographical tool for Aegean studies, Homeric society, and Indo‑European linguistics and does not itself provide detailed information on the findspot or acquisition history of the Ring of Nestor. [29]

- At least one modern paper, listed as ‘Some observations on the iconography of the “ring of nestor”’, analyses the ring’s imagery within wider debates on Aegean forgeries and authenticity and likely addresses its collection history and Evans’s claim of a Thisbe, Boeotia origin, though its detailed arguments and conclusions remain unverified in the present evidence set. [4]

- Overall, on the basis of Evans’s narrative and its transmission in modern bibliographic sources, the best‑supported working provenance for the Ring of Nestor is as a market‑acquired object said to belong to a sepulchral treasure of gold signet rings and bead‑seals from a Boeotian tomb at or near Thisbe, while associations with Pylos function as literary and comparative frames rather than documented findspot claims; nonetheless, the reliance on antiquities‑market information means that the Thisbe attribution cannot be regarded as securely verified. [20][27][28]
