# Ring of Nestor

The Ring of Nestor is the name used in archaeological literature for a gold signet ring associated with Aegean Bronze Age studies and identified in scholarly discussions as CMS VI, no. 277.[1][2] Its publication history begins with Sir Arthur Evans's 1925 study, *The Ring of Nestor: A Glimpse into the Minoan After-World and a Sepulchral Treasure of Gold Signet-Rings and Bead-Seals from Thisbê, Boeotia*; a related notice by V. Gordon Childe appeared in *Nature* in 1925.[3][4] Evans interpreted the scene on the bezel as representing a Minoan Elysian domain.[5]

The reported provenance is not consistent across the surviving accounts. Evans's account connected the associated treasure with a Mycenaean rock-cut tomb near Thisbê in Boeotia, with a find date of 1915, whereas another scholarly account describes the ring as allegedly found in a tholos tomb at Kakovatos near Pylos; a separate abstract gives 1924 as the find year.[6][7][8] The authenticity of the object has consequently remained disputed: Evans accepted it as genuine, while other assessments have treated it as a modern forgery; later publications have again argued both for and against authenticity, including arguments based on Egyptian prototypes and the butterfly motif.[9][10]

## Discovery and provenance

## Discovery and provenance

The earliest published account links the object to a group of gold rings and gold bead-seals found in a Mycenaean rock tomb near Thisbê in Boeotia. The account dates the find to 1915 and describes it as having occurred during wartime conditions, when discovery was diverted from official archaeological channels. [13] It states that a peasant found the material in a rock-cut chamber tomb near the village of Dombrena. W. A. Heurtley was shown three chamber tombs with dromoi in an olive grove, but the published extract withholds some details on grounds of confidentiality and does not establish that the treasure came from all three tombs. [13]

This account differs from a later description that calls the provenance an alleged discovery in a tholos tomb at Kakovatos near Pylos. The difference cannot be reconciled from the supplied evidence. The more cautious conclusion is that the ring has a reported, but not decisively documented, archaeological provenance; the 2019 discussion explicitly places it among objects whose affiliation with Bronze Age material is weakened by the absence of professional, well-documented excavations. [9] [13]

The ring first appeared in scholarly literature in 1925. By that time Arthur Evans had privately purchased it and published it in the *Journal of Hellenic Studies*. He also issued a separate London edition, *The Ring of Nestor: A Glimpse into the Minoan After-World and a Sepulchral Treasure of Gold Signet-Rings and Bead-Seals from Thisbê, Boeotia*, published by Macmillan in 1925; the National Library of Ireland catalog records that the book was issued separately, by permission, from an April 1925 paper in the journal. [9] [11] The supplied institutional catalog records concern copies of Evans's book, not a documented chain of custody or current institutional holder for the physical ring. [11] [12]

## Description and iconography

## Description and iconography

The Ring of Nestor is described as a gold signet ring and is identified in the supplied scholarly material as CMS VI, no. 277. A review of the Ashmolean seal corpus groups catalogue numbers 277-281 among gold rings with cult scenes. [9] [10] The supplied material does not provide a securely attributable physical measurement for no. 277. Measurements of 2.1 cm by 1.2 cm are explicitly assigned to no. 278, while an AI-generated summary gives 3.29 cm by 2.18 cm for the Nestor ring without an accompanying original-text verification. [10] [21] The mass of 31.8 g and the inventory number AN1938.1130 are associated with an Ashmolean object in secondary catalog data, but the same data give CMS VI.229 rather than CMS VI.277, so these details cannot safely be treated as a verified description of the ring discussed here. [22] [23]

Evans interpreted the imagery on the bezel as a Minoan Elysian domain, an afterlife scene. [9] The later iconographic discussion summarized in the supplied material identifies a key figure previously called Evans's "watch-dog" or "dragon" as a scorpion and treats it as an apotropaic symbol in Bronze Age Aegean iconography. [6] Another study, by Nanno Marinatos and Briana Jackson, relates the image's coherence to Egyptian prototypes and argues that the use of a butterfly motif weighs against authenticity. [2] The supplied record also contains an opposing 2013 iconographic argument associated with Theodore Eliopoulos, but the available text is a landing-page summary rather than the paper's full analysis; its claim that incurved altars support authenticity therefore requires caution. [3]

A photograph caption identifies a Ring of Nestor displayed at the Ashmolean Museum in Oxford in 2025, with the Ashmolean named as the image source, but the caption is not a full curatorial record. [7] The Ashmolean corpus review also records an editorial duplication in which a photograph of the Nestor ring appears on a page devoted to Evans's replicas of the Ring of Minos. [10]

## Authenticity debate

## Authenticity debate

The authenticity question was present from the object's entry into modern scholarship. The 1925 publication associated with Sir Arthur Evans records his acceptance of the ring as authentic, while later summaries report that other scholars condemned it as a forgery.[1][2] A discussion of the object's early history states that it first appeared in scholarly literature in 1925 after Evans had privately purchased it and published it in the *Journal of Hellenic Studies*. The same account reports that members of the archaeological board in Athens had refused to buy it from its owner because they considered it the work of a modern artist.[3] These reports document competing historical judgments, but they do not by themselves establish the object's authenticity or falsity.

Arguments against authenticity have focused on both provenance and iconography. Nanno Marinatos and Briana Jackson, in their 2011 article, characterize the circumstances of the find as suspicious and argue that the composition depends on Egyptian prototypes; they further state that the use of the butterfly motif weighs against authenticity.[4][5] Their abstract does not reproduce the detailed comparative analysis or specify all of the circumstances on which the judgment rests. The absence of a professionally documented excavation context has also been treated as a reason for caution in assessing the ring's attribution to Bronze Age material.[3]

Arguments in favor of authenticity have been advanced through later iconographic reassessment. An abstract of A. G. Vlachopoulos's 2020 study identifies the object as CMS VI, no. 277 and describes a reassessment of cases in which Cretan-Mycenaean gold rings had been erroneously classified as fakes; the abstract states that comparable wall paintings, gold rings, figurines and ivories contributed to that reassessment.[6] Vlachopoulos also proposes that Evans's "watch-dog" or "dragon" should be identified as a scorpion, an apotropaic symbol in Bronze Age Aegean iconography.[7] These arguments represent a scholarly interpretation rather than a conclusive scientific authentication. The available record therefore supports describing the Ring of Nestor as an object whose ancient attribution and authenticity remain unresolved.
